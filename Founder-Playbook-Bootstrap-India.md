## 0. Read this first: your situation is not the report's situation

The market report answers *"what is the best business in this market?"* It answers that for a team with **$500K–1M and a clinical co-founder**. You have neither. That is not a small difference — it changes the first eighteen months completely.

### 0.1 What the report assumed vs. what is true for you

| The report assumed | Your reality | What changes |
|---|---|---|
| ₹4–8 crore to reach first evidence | ₹0 | Cannot build software, cannot run a study, cannot register a company comfortably |
| A clinical co-founder or advisor | No clinical access at all | Cannot make clinical claims, cannot sell to clinics credibly, cannot run a protocol |
| India for validation, US for revenue | India only | Revenue per patient is 5× lower; the US step moves to year 3+, or disappears |
| Venture-scale ambition tolerated | Profitable, self-owned | Raising VC actively harms this goal (§9) |
| Software-first | Service-first, ideally no software | The first "product" is your time and a spreadsheet |

**The honest consequence:** you cannot execute the market report's recommendation in year one. What you *can* do is spend year one acquiring the three things you lack — **clinical access, a paying customer, and domain credibility** — at zero cost, and then execute a narrower version of it from a position of strength.

This is not a detour. It is the only path that doesn't end with a dead company and a personal loan.

### 0.2 The one question that decides your next 90 days

> **Can you get one clinic, lab, or corporate to pay you ₹15,000–50,000 for a small, defined piece of work — before you spend ₹1 building anything?**

If yes: you have a business, and everything in this playbook applies.
If no: you have a hypothesis, and you need Route 1 (§1.2) — get paid to learn inside the system first.

Everything else — the deck, the cap table, the product, the grants — is downstream of that single transaction.

### 0.3 What "profitable, self-owned" actually means in numbers

Because it changes every decision, here is the target stated plainly:

| Milestone | Revenue | Team | Timeline (realistic) |
|---|---|---|---|
| First rupee | ₹15–50K one-off | You | Month 2–4 |
| Ramen profitable | ₹1.5–3L/month | You + 1 part-time | Month 9–15 |
| Real business | ₹50L–1Cr/year, 40%+ margin | 3–5 people | Year 2–3 |
| Comfortable | ₹2–5Cr/year | 8–15 people | Year 4–6 |

**No outside capital required at any of those levels.** This is the single most important structural fact about your chosen outcome: it is *achievable without permission from investors*. It is not achievable quickly, and it is not achievable while also chasing a ₹500Cr valuation.

### 0.4 The five things that will actually kill you

Not competition. Not Apple. These:

1. **You run out of personal money before revenue.** Mitigation: keep income, even part-time, until month 12.
2. **No clinician will take your calls.** Mitigation: Route 1 or the access channels in §4; this is a 6-month problem, not a permanent one.
3. **You build software nobody asked for.** Mitigation: the manual-first rule (§6) — never automate a process you haven't delivered by hand for money.
4. **You sell to individuals instead of institutions.** Consumers in India pay ₹26 for a smartwatch band and churn in 5 weeks [MR S10]; clinics and labs have budgets and repeat demand.
5. **You confuse activity with progress.** A deck, a logo, a website, and 40 "great conversations" are worth zero. One signed purchase order is worth everything.

## 1. Three routes that fit ₹0 — and the one to take

Only three routes are honestly available to someone with no capital and no clinical network. Two of them look like compromises and aren't. The fourth option people default to — "build an app and see" — is not on the list because it cannot work here: an app with no clinical buyer, no evidence, and no distribution has no path to revenue, and a health app with clinical claims has a regulatory problem before it has a customer.

### 1.1 Route comparison

| | **R1. Get paid to learn** | **R2. Concierge service** | **R3. Partner/reseller** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **What it is** | Take a job or contract inside a diagnostics chain, hospital ops, healthtech company, or corporate health team | Sell a small, manually delivered BP follow-up / confirmation service to one institution | Become the India implementation or sales partner for an existing ABPM or RPM vendor |
| **Capital needed** | ₹0 | ₹0–25K (travel, phone, printing) | ₹0 |
| **Time to first rupee** | 30–45 days (salary) | 45–90 days | 60–120 days (commission) |
| **Clinical access gained** | **High** — you sit inside the system daily | Medium — through your buyer's clinicians | Medium — vendor's clinical team |
| **Domain learning** | High but slow | **Highest per month** — you touch the actual workflow | Medium |
| **Risk** | Low | Medium (your time, some ego) | Low |
| **Ceiling on its own** | Low (you're an employee) | **High (this is the company)** | Low (you're a channel) |
| **Best for** | Someone who needs income in 60 days, or has zero network and needs one | Someone with 15+ hours/week and tolerance for rejection | Someone with sales experience and existing B2B contacts |

### 1.2 R1 — Get paid to learn (do not dismiss this)

With zero capital, you almost certainly need income. The wrong response is to pretend otherwise and burn three months of savings. The right response is to take a job that **pays you to build the exact assets you need**: clinical relationships, workflow knowledge, and a view of where money actually moves.

**Target employers, in order of usefulness for this specific thesis:**

1. **Diagnostic lab chains with home-collection networks** (Dr Lal PathLabs, Metropolis, Thyrocare, Redcliffe, state-level chains). Reason: they already send phlebotomists into homes, they already sell chronic-disease packages, and BP is the obvious adjacent measurement. You will learn the exact operational constraint your future product removes.
2. **Hospital chain operations / quality teams.** Reason: you see how clinical protocols actually get enforced (or not), and you meet clinicians as colleagues rather than prospects.
3. **RPM / chronic-care startups.** Reason: fastest domain learning, but the smallest salary and the highest chance the company itself fails.
4. **Corporate health / insurance wellness teams.** Reason: you meet the payer side, which is where the money eventually comes from.

**What to extract in 12 months, explicitly:** 20 clinician relationships, one understanding of how a clinic buys anything, one understanding of how BP is actually measured in Indian primary care, and one written case study you can show a future customer. Treat the job as paid market research with a salary attached.

**Time box:** 12–18 months. Not five years. Write the exit date down.

### 1.3 R2 — The concierge service (recommended)

This is the one to run, in parallel with R1 if necessary.

**What you sell:** a *BP follow-up desk* for one institution. Not an app. Not a device. A person (you) plus a documented process.

**What the buyer gets, concretely:**
- Every patient who leaves with a high BP reading gets a structured 7-day home measurement series, with reminders and a follow-up call.
- Results are compiled into a one-page summary, in the clinic's format, delivered to the treating doctor before the follow-up visit.
- Patients who don't respond get escalated by phone, not lost.
- The institution gets a monthly report: how many patients were flagged, how many completed, how many came back, how many started or changed treatment.

**Why an institution pays for this:**
- It fills empty follow-up slots (revenue per slot).
- It converts one-off lab/OPD visits into chronic-care relationships (lifetime value per patient).
- It is auditable, which matters for accreditation and for corporate contracts.
- It requires no new software, no capex, and no regulatory approval from them.

**What it costs you to deliver:** a phone, WhatsApp Business, a Google Sheet, a printed protocol card, and 3–4 hours per patient cohort. That's it. Total cash: under ₹25,000.

**Why this is the right first product even though it is unglamorous:** it is the *only* version of this business you can sell before you can build, and every rupee of it teaches you the workflow you would otherwise guess at. Software written after 50 manual deliveries is 10× better than software written before.

**Pricing:** ₹300–800 per patient for a 7-day series with a summary, or ₹15,000–40,000/month for a fixed cohort of 50–100 patients. Start with a **paid pilot**: ₹15,000 for 30 patients over 6 weeks, paid upfront. If they won't pay ₹15,000, they will not pay ₹1.5 lakh later, and you have saved yourself a year.

### 1.4 R3 — Partner/reseller

Become the India channel for a company that already has the device and the regulatory clearance — an ABPM vendor, a home-BP device maker, an RPM platform. You earn 15–30% commission, you borrow their credibility, and you learn enterprise sales on someone else's product.

**Realistic assessment:** good for cash and contacts, weak as a company (you don't own the customer or the IP). Use it as a bridge, not a destination. The specific advantage: vendors will happily train you, give you clinical materials, and introduce you to their hospital customers, because you're making them money.

### 1.5 The recommendation for your profile

> **Run R2 as the company, with R1 as the income bridge if needed, and use R3 tactically to borrow credibility in month 1–3.**

Concretely:
- **Month 1:** apply to 10 diagnostic-chain roles (R1 insurance) *and* start the R2 customer conversations. Do not wait for either.
- **Month 2–3:** one paid R2 pilot. Any size. The point is the signature, not the amount.
- **Month 4–6:** three pilots, one case study, one clinician who will take your call.
- **Month 6–9:** decide — is this a business, or was it a lesson?

### 1.6 What is rejected, and why

| Rejected path | Why it fails for you specifically |
|---|---|
| **Build an app first** | No buyer, no distribution, no evidence, and if it makes clinical claims it is a regulated device with a ₹2–8 lakh compliance path. You would spend your only resource (time) on the one thing that doesn't produce revenue. |
| **Consumer/D2C product** | Indian consumer willingness to pay is ₹1,500–3,000 for hardware with a $26 ASP market [MR S10]; support costs exceed margin; churn is brutal. |
| **Hardware** | Requires ₹5–30 crore and 24 months before revenue [MR §8.2]. Not a bootstrap path under any scenario. |
| **Grant-first** | Every meaningful grant requires a registered company, an incubator, and often a technical founder (§7.2). You cannot start there; you can end up there. |
| **Content/community first** | Slow, no defensible asset, and the audience you'd build (health-conscious consumers) is not the customer you need (institutions). |
| **Franchise / clinic ownership** | Capex-heavy, regulated, and it makes you a clinic operator rather than a product company. |

## 2. The legal line: what you may do without a medical degree or a device licence

This is the chapter most startup advice omits, and getting it wrong is the fastest way to end the company before it starts.

### 2.1 The three things you must never do

1. **Diagnose.** You cannot tell a person they have hypertension, or that they don't.
2. **Prescribe or advise treatment.** You cannot say "increase your dose" or "stop your medicine."
3. **Present a software output as a clinical conclusion.** A number that says "you have stage 2 hypertension" is a diagnostic claim.

A non-clinician doing any of these is practising medicine without registration. No amount of "it's just an app" language changes that.

### 2.2 What you may do, freely

| Activity | Status | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Measure blood pressure with a validated device | **Allowed** | Measurement is not diagnosis; anyone can operate a validated cuff |
| Remind patients to measure, and collect the readings | **Allowed** | Administrative / logistics |
| Compile readings into a summary for the doctor | **Allowed** | You are formatting data the clinician will interpret |
| Schedule follow-up appointments | **Allowed** | Pure logistics |
| Educate patients using material approved by the clinic | **Allowed** | The clinic owns the content and the claim |
| Train clinic staff on a measurement protocol written by a clinician | **Allowed** | The protocol is the clinician's; you are the trainer |
| Report programme metrics to the institution | **Allowed** | Business reporting |
| Tell a patient their reading is "high, please see the doctor" | **Allowed with care** | This mirrors what a pharmacy or a home nurse does; keep it to "outside the range the clinic set, please contact them" |
| Interpret a trend and recommend a treatment change | **Not allowed** | Clinical decision |
| Generate a risk score or "diagnosis" from data | **Not allowed** without device regulation | This is SaMD |

**The design rule that keeps you legal:** *the clinician owns every clinical decision; you own the pathway that gets the patient to that decision.* Say this in every sales meeting, write it into every contract, and put it in the product.

### 2.3 When software tips you into regulation

The line is not "software vs. no software." It is **what the software concludes**.

| Software does this | Regulatory status |
|---|---|
| Stores readings, charts them, reminds the patient, sends the doctor a PDF | **Not a medical device** — record-keeping and communication |
| Flags "outside the range your doctor set" | Borderline; safest if the threshold is set by the clinician, not by you |
| Says "likely hypertension", "your BP is uncontrolled", "increase medication" | **Medical device** (SaMD) → CDSCO licensing [MR S23] |
| Predicts risk, triages, or recommends a pathway | **Medical device**, and in the EU Class IIa under Rule 11 [MR S22] |

So your safe product surface for the first two years is: **capture, remind, format, route.** That is genuinely most of the value, and it is unregulated.

### 2.4 The contracts you need on day one (and what they cost)

You do not need a lawyer for the first pilot, but you do need three documents. Templates exist; a CA/lawyer review of a 3-page agreement costs ₹5,000–15,000 and is worth it before your second customer.

1. **Service agreement with the institution.** Scope, per-patient fee, payment terms (50% upfront), data ownership (the institution owns the patient record; you are a processor), liability cap, termination, and an explicit clause: *"The Company provides administrative and logistical services only. All clinical decisions, including diagnosis and treatment, are made solely by the Institution's qualified clinicians."*
2. **Data processing agreement** (can be a schedule inside #1). Under India's DPDP Act and Rules 2025 [MR S24], you are handling health data: you need purpose limitation, a consent notice the institution gives the patient, retention rules, breach notification, and a named contact. Write it in plain English; regulators care about substance, not length.
3. **Patient consent / information sheet**, in the local language, approved by the institution's clinician. It must say what is collected, who sees it, how long it is kept, and that participation does not replace a doctor's advice.

**What you must NOT do at this stage:** sign anything that makes you the clinical provider, promise outcomes, or accept a contract where the institution disclaims all responsibility for clinical decisions while you carry the liability.

### 2.5 Invoicing, GST, and the company question

| Question | Answer for a ₹0 start |
|---|---|
| Do I need a company to invoice? | No. A sole proprietorship with your PAN can invoice. Many institutions will accept it for small pilots; some require a GSTIN. |
| Do I need GST? | Mandatory above ₹20 lakh/year turnover for services. Register voluntarily earlier if a customer requires it — it costs nothing and makes you look institutional. |
| Do I need a Pvt Ltd now? | **No — and incorporating early can hurt you** (§7.2: SISFS requires incorporation within 2 years, so the clock starts when you register). Incorporate when you have either ₹5–10 lakh of contracted revenue, a co-founder, or a grant application that requires it. |
| What does incorporation cost? | ₹7,000–15,000 including professional fees for a Pvt Ltd via SPICe+; an LLP is cheaper but worse for future equity. |
| Can I take money personally? | Yes, as proprietorship income, taxed at slab. Keep a separate bank account from day one — mixing personal and business money is the single most common bookkeeping disaster. |

### 2.6 Liability: the ₹5,000 decision that protects you

Take **professional indemnity insurance** before your first patient is enrolled. For a non-clinical service business it is inexpensive (₹5,000–15,000/year for a small cover) and it is the difference between a mistake being a bad week and a bad decade. Ask for a policy that covers "administrative and logistical services in healthcare" — not medical malpractice, which you don't need and can't get.

## 3. The first 90 days, week by week

Assumption: you have 10–15 hours/week if employed, or 40 if not. Every task below is free or under ₹2,000. Nothing here requires a company, a lawyer, or a line of code.

### 3.1 Week 1–2: Build the target list (not the product)

**Do this:** write down 40 institutions within a 90-minute commute. Not 400 — 40, with names, phone numbers, and the name of one person who works there.

| Target type | How many | Why they're on the list |
|---|---|---|
| Standalone pathology labs (3–10 collection centres) | 12 | Already do home visits, want chronic-disease packages, low regulatory friction |
| Polyclinics / 2–5 doctor practices | 10 | High BP volume, follow-up is their revenue problem |
| Physiotherapy / cardiac rehab centres | 6 | BP monitoring is clinically necessary and they have no system for it |
| Diagnostic chain branch managers | 6 | Gateway to a chain-level contract |
| Corporate wellness providers | 3 | Have B2B contracts and need content |
| Home nursing / homecare companies | 3 | Already in homes daily; BP is the obvious add-on |

**How to find them:** Google Maps (search "pathology lab near me", "physiotherapy clinic"), Practo/Justdial listings, LinkedIn for branch managers and lab owners, and — most effective — walk into three labs and ask who runs the place.

**Output of week 2:** a spreadsheet with 40 rows: name, type, contact, decision-maker name, last contact date, next action, status.

### 3.2 Week 2–3: The 15-minute conversation

Do not pitch. Ask five questions and shut up. The script is deliberately short because long scripts signal desperation.

> **Opening (phone or in person):**
> "Hello, I'm [name]. I'm working on a small project about how clinics follow up with patients who have high blood pressure readings. I'm not selling anything today — I'm trying to understand how it actually works in practice. Could I have 15 minutes this week?"
>
> **The five questions:**
> 1. "When a patient's BP is high in your clinic, what happens next — who follows up, and when?"
> 2. "How do you know whether they actually measured it at home, or came back?"
> 3. "What happens to the ones who don't come back?"
> 4. "If someone compiled the home readings into a one-page summary before the follow-up visit, would that change what the doctor does?"
> 5. "Who in your organisation would decide to try something like that?"
>
> **Close:**
> "That's very useful. Would it be worth 20 minutes to show you what a 30-patient version would look like — at no cost for the first 10 patients, so you can see whether it's useful?"

**What you are listening for:** the phrase *"we don't have anyone for that."* That is the entire business. If you hear it three times in ten conversations, proceed. If you hear "the doctor already does it" ten times, the problem is smaller than the report suggests, and you have learned that for free — which is the point.

### 3.3 Week 3–4: Build the thing you'll sell (it takes 2 days)

Deliverable: a **two-page document** you can leave behind.

- **Page 1:** "The 7-day BP confirmation programme." What it is, who it's for, exactly what happens day by day, what the doctor receives, what it costs.
- **Page 2:** the one-page summary the doctor would receive, filled in with a fake but realistic patient (label it clearly as an example).

That's it. No app. No logo. No website. A document that shows the output is worth more than a prototype, because the doctor is buying the summary, not the software.

**Cost:** ₹0–500 to print 20 copies. Print them. Physical leave-behinds get kept; PDFs get ignored.

### 3.4 Week 4–6: Convert one paid pilot

**The offer, exactly:**

> "30 patients over 6 weeks. ₹15,000, paid upfront. You give me a list of patients with a high reading and their consent. I run the 7-day series, chase the ones who drop off, and give your doctor a one-page summary before each follow-up. If fewer than 20 patients complete the series, you don't pay the second half."

**Why this structure:**
- **Paid** — free pilots teach you nothing about willingness to pay and get deprioritised by the clinic.
- **Small** — ₹15,000 is below the threshold where a clinic needs three approvals.
- **Outcome-linked** — you take the completion risk, which is the risk you control.
- **Upfront** — cash before cost, so you never fund a clinic's working capital.

**If they say no:** ask one question — "Is it the price, the process, or the timing?" Then log the answer. Twenty nos with reasons is a better asset than one yes with no reason.

### 3.5 Week 6–12: Deliver, measure, document

Get 30 patients through. Then measure exactly four things:

| Metric | Target | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Enrolment rate (of patients offered, how many joined) | >60% | Whether the clinic's staff can actually recruit, or whether you must do it |
| **Series completion rate** (7 days of readings captured) | >55% | The single hardest operational number; if this is below 40%, the whole model is in question |
| Doctor action rate (summary led to a documented decision) | >50% | Whether the output is useful or decorative |
| Staff time saved / added | Net saving | The commercial argument in one number |

**Then write a two-page case study:** what was done, the four numbers, one doctor quote, one patient quote (with consent), and what you'd change. This document is worth more than a pitch deck for your next five customers, because it is evidence rather than assertion.

### 3.6 Day 90: the decision

| Evidence | Decision |
|---|---|
| 1 paid pilot + completion >55% + doctor action >50% | **Continue.** Raise the price, add 2 more sites. |
| 1 paid pilot but completion <40% | **Fix the process once.** Change the reminder cadence, shorten to 5 days, add a phone call at day 2. Re-run with 30 patients. |
| 3+ pilots, no payment | **Stop selling, go back to Route 1.** The demand is not there at this price, and a job inside the system will teach you why faster than another 40 conversations. |
| 0 pilots, 20 conversations | **Your access is the problem, not the product.** Spend 90 days in Route 1 and come back with colleagues instead of cold calls. |

## 4. Getting clinical access from zero

This is your binding constraint, more than money. Seven channels, ranked by how well they work for a stranger with no credentials.

### 4.1 Ranked channels

| # | Channel | Why it works | Effort | Realistic yield |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Work inside a diagnostic chain or hospital** | Proximity beats persuasion; you become a colleague, not a vendor | High (a job) | 20 relationships in 12 months |
| 2 | **Physiotherapy / cardiac rehab centres** | Less hierarchy, owner is often the clinician, BP monitoring is genuinely needed | Low | 2–3 receptive clinicians in 20 calls |
| 3 | **Standalone pathology lab owners** | Owner decides alone, wants chronic-disease upsell, understands per-patient economics | Low | 1 pilot in 15 conversations |
| 4 | **Medical college community-medicine departments** | They need field data and study support; you offer free operational help | Medium | 1 advisor + access to residents |
| 5 | **Local IMA branch meetings / CME events** | Informal access to 30 clinicians in one evening; low cost | Low | 3–5 conversations per event |
| 6 | **Pharma medical representatives** | They know every doctor's name and temperament, and they trade favours | Low, but transactional | 5 warm intros in a month |
| 7 | **LinkedIn cold outreach to doctors** | Works, but the lowest response rate (~5–10%) | Low | 1 conversation per 20 messages |

### 4.2 What actually gets a clinician to talk to you

Five things, in order of effectiveness:

1. **Offer free labour, specifically.** "I'll run the first 10 patients myself at no cost, and you'll get the summaries whether or not you continue." Clinicians are time-poor, not money-poor in this context.
2. **Bring a document, not a pitch.** A one-page protocol and an example summary signals you've done work. A deck signals you want something.
3. **Speak their language, once.** Say "ambulatory BP", "white-coat hypertension", "masked hypertension", "titration", "target organ damage". Then stop. Over-using jargon marks you as a salesperson; using it once marks you as prepared.
4. **Name the guideline.** Indian hypertension management follows ICMR/API guidance; international reference is the 2025 AHA/ACC update. Saying "the guideline says confirm before starting treatment" makes your service a *compliance* tool rather than an extra cost.
5. **Never claim clinical benefit you can't evidence.** One overclaim and you are a vendor forever.

### 4.3 The advisor ask (when you're ready, month 3–6)

Once you've run one pilot, ask the most engaged clinician:

> "Would you be willing to be a clinical advisor? It's 2 hours a month — review the protocol, take a call, and let me use your name with other clinics. I can't pay cash yet, but I can offer [X]% advisory equity vesting over 2 years, or a fixed fee once we have revenue."

**Advisory equity norms in India:** 0.25–1% for a genuine advisor, vesting over 24 months with a 6-month cliff, documented in a simple advisor agreement with IP assignment. Never give advisory equity before they've done anything; make the first 3 months unpaid and unvested.

**What to never do:** ask a clinician to be a "co-founder" before you have a product or revenue. You will give away 30% for a signature.

## 5. First revenue: pricing, pilot mechanics, and what to say when they object

### 5.1 Pricing, with the arithmetic

Your cost to deliver 30 patients over 6 weeks: ~25 hours of your time, ₹500 of phone/data, ₹2,000 of printing and travel. Cash cost: **~₹2,500**.

| Price | Gross margin | Signal it sends |
|---|---|---|
| ₹5,000 | 50% | "This is a hobby" — and it anchors you permanently low |
| **₹15,000** | **83%** | **Serious but small enough to approve** |
| ₹30,000 | 92% | Needs a manager's sign-off; slower, but filters for real intent |
| ₹50,000+ | 95% | Requires proof; do not attempt before your second case study |

**Rule:** never discount the price; reduce the scope. "₹15,000 for 30 patients" is better than "₹7,500 for 30 patients," because the second sets a reference point you cannot escape.

### 5.2 The six objections you will hear, and the answers

| Objection | What it usually means | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| "We already tell patients to check at home." | They have no follow-up mechanism | "Who calls the ones who don't? I'll take that part, and you'll see how many were actually lost." |
| "Our doctors are too busy to review summaries." | The output format is wrong | "The summary is one page, three lines, and sits on top of the file. If it takes more than 30 seconds, I'll change it." |
| "Patient data can't leave our system." | Legitimate DPDP concern | "It doesn't. I work inside your premises or your WhatsApp Business number, under your consent process, and you own the record. Here's the clause." |
| "We tried a vendor and it didn't work." | Prior failure, usually engagement | "What did they ask your staff to do? My version asks staff to do nothing except hand over a card." |
| "Come back next quarter." | Polite no | "Understood. Can I run 10 patients free in the meantime so there's something concrete to look at next quarter?" |
| "What's your background?" | Trust gap, not a dealbreaker | Be honest. "I'm not a clinician. I run the logistics; your doctor makes every clinical decision. That's exactly why the protocol is written by you." |

### 5.3 What not to promise

- **Do not promise lower BP.** You cannot control treatment, adherence, or physiology.
- **Do not promise "early detection."** With 41.2% sensitivity in consumer screening [MR S13, S14], detection claims are indefensible and invite the wrong comparison.
- **Do not promise cost savings** you haven't measured in their clinic.
- **Do promise** completion rates, turnaround time, documentation quality, and staff time saved — the four things you actually control.

### 5.4 When to raise the price

After the second site renews at the pilot price, move to a monthly retainer:

| Stage | Structure | Price |
|---|---|---|
| Pilot | Fixed cohort, one-off | ₹15,000 / 30 patients |
| Repeat | Monthly cohort | ₹25,000–40,000 / month (50–80 patients) |
| Multi-site | Per-patient, volume-tiered | ₹350–500 / patient |
| Chain | Annual contract, SLA | ₹4–12 lakh / year |

The jump from pilot to retainer is where most first-time founders lose the customer: they keep selling pilots because pilots are easy to say yes to. Ask for the retainer at the moment the case study lands, not three months later.

## 6. From service to product: the automation ladder

The mistake that kills service-first founders is automating too early. The second mistake is never automating and becoming a permanent freelancer. The ladder below has explicit triggers.

### 6.1 The four rungs

| Rung | What it is | Cash cost | Trigger to climb |
|---|---|---|---|
| **0. Manual** | WhatsApp + Google Sheets + a Word template. You do everything by hand. | ₹0 | — |
| **1. Templated** | Fixed Google Sheet with validation, a Docs template with mail-merge, a saved WhatsApp message library | ₹0–1,500 | 10 paying cohorts delivered |
| **2. No-code** | A form for intake, an automation for reminders, a dashboard for the clinic | ₹3,000–8,000/month | 3 paying sites, and you are turning work away |
| **3. Custom software** | Your own app/portal, multi-tenant, audit logs | ₹4–15 lakh build | 8–10 paying sites and a repeatable sales motion |
| **4. Regulated product** | Clinical decision support with CDSCO clearance | ₹25 lakh+ | You have evidence, revenue, and a clinical partner |

**The rule:** never climb a rung until the rung below is *boring*. If delivery still surprises you weekly, the process isn't ready to be encoded — you'll encode the wrong thing, which is more expensive than doing it by hand.

### 6.2 What to automate first (in this order)

1. **The doctor's summary.** It is the thing the customer pays for; a mail-merge from a structured sheet takes 10 seconds instead of 20 minutes. Highest value per rupee of effort.
2. **The reminder sequence.** Day 1, 2, 4, 6 messages. This is a scheduled message library, not software.
3. **The drop-off list.** Who hasn't responded in 48 hours. A filtered view, not an app.
4. **Intake and consent.** A form with the consent notice embedded.
5. **Everything else.** Billing, contracts, reporting — these stay manual longest and should.

**What not to build, ever, as a bootstrapper:** a patient-facing app. Patients will not download it for a 7-day programme. WhatsApp is the app, and it is already installed.

### 6.3 The ₹4–15 lakh software question

If you reach rung 3, you face a real decision: build or buy. Honest options:

| Option | Cost | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Freelancer in India (1 dev, 3–4 months) | ₹4–8 lakh | High — single point of failure, no maintenance |
| Small agency | ₹8–15 lakh | Medium — better process, still no ongoing ownership |
| Offshore team (Vietnam, Eastern Europe) | ₹6–12 lakh | Medium — timezone, communication |
| Technical co-founder | 15–30% equity | **Best if you can find one** — but only after you have revenue and a validated process |
| Buy a white-label RPM platform | ₹5,000–20,000/month | Low risk, low differentiation |

**Recommendation:** at rung 3, buy or rent before you build. A white-label platform plus your process and relationships is a real business. Custom software is a moat only when the workflow is genuinely unusual — and yours is not unusual yet.

## 7. Money without investors

You have four sources of capital available, and the order matters more than the amounts.

### 7.1 The bootstrap capital stack, in priority order

| Priority | Source | Amount | Cost of capital | When |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Customer prepayment** | ₹15K–2L | Free (you deliver later) | Month 1 onwards |
| 2 | **Grants** | ₹10–50L | Free but slow and restricted | Month 9–24 |
| 3 | **Customer-investors** (clinic owners, lab promoters, doctors) | ₹10–50L | 8–15% equity, plus a customer | Month 12–24 |
| 4 | **Revenue-based / working capital loan** | ₹5–25L | 14–18% interest | Month 18+ |
| — | **VC** | ₹3–15Cr | 20–30% equity + control | Only if you reverse your goal (§7.5) |

**Why customer prepayment is first:** it is the only capital that comes with validation attached. A ₹15,000 advance proves demand; a ₹50 lakh angel cheque proves only that someone likes you.

### 7.2 Grants: what actually exists, and why they're not your first money

Two schemes are relevant, and both have eligibility rules that trip up bootstrappers.

**BIRAC Biotechnology Ignition Grant (BIG)** — up to **₹50 lakh** grant-in-aid over **18 months**, for proof-of-concept in healthcare, diagnostics, devices, and life sciences. Calls open **twice a year (1 January and 1 July)** and stay open ~45 days. Requirements: an Indian company or LLP incorporated **within the last 5 years**, physical incubation at a recognised incubator, an in-house R&D facility, and for individual applicants a relevant PhD/MBBS/engineering background [S-G1].

**Startup India Seed Fund Scheme (SISFS)** — up to **₹20 lakh grant** for proof of concept, prototype or product trials, plus up to **₹50 lakh** via convertible debentures or debt for commercialisation. Requires **DPIIT recognition**, incorporation **within the last 2 years**, and disbursement through an approved incubator. **Individual entrepreneurs are not eligible** [S-G2].

**Three consequences you must plan around:**

1. **Grants fund technology, not services.** A BP follow-up desk run on WhatsApp is not an R&D project. You become eligible when you have a software product to build or a clinical study to run — month 9–18, not month 1.
2. **Incorporation starts a clock.** SISFS requires incorporation within 2 years; BIG within 5. Register too early and you burn eligibility before you're ready to apply; register too late and you miss the window. The sweet spot is registering 3–6 months before your first serious application.
3. **Grants reimburse, they don't advance.** You generally spend first and claim against milestones, with utilisation certificates and audited statements. A ₹50 lakh grant does not solve a cash-flow problem for someone with ₹0.

**Also worth knowing:** DPIIT recognition is free, takes 2–4 weeks, and unlocks a 3-year income-tax holiday under 80-IAC (subject to approval), self-certification of compliance, and access to most schemes. Do it when you incorporate; it costs nothing.

**Realistic grant timeline for you:**

```
Month 0–9    Build revenue + software prototype
Month 9–12   Incorporate, DPIIT recognition, join an incubator
Month 12     Apply to SISFS round (rolling) and BIG (1 Jan or 1 Jul)
Month 14–18  Award decision; disbursement in milestones
```

### 7.3 Customer-investors: the right first cheque

The best small cheque for your business is not from an angel network. It is from **someone who will also become a customer**: a diagnostic-chain promoter, a clinic owner, a hospital administrator, a corporate health buyer.

**Why it works:** they understand the problem, they bring distribution, and they don't need a 10× exit thesis to say yes. A ₹15–25 lakh cheque for 10–15% at a ₹1.5–2.5 crore valuation is realistic at ₹30–50 lakh ARR.

**Structure in India:**

| Instrument | Use when | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **CCPS** (compulsorily convertible preference shares) | Standard equity round | Most investor-friendly, clean cap table; requires valuation report and MCA filings |
| **CCD** (compulsorily convertible debentures) | SISFS commercialisation tranche | Debt that converts; simpler in some ways, but interest accrues |
| **Convertible note / iSAFE-style** | Very early, terms unsettled | Cheapest to execute; must be drafted carefully to be enforceable in India; cap + discount |
| **Simple equity + shareholders' agreement** | When you want governance clarity | Adds cost (₹25–50K legal) but avoids surprises |

**Never do:** a handshake equity deal, equity for "advisory" without vesting, or a convertible note with no valuation cap and no maturity date. Each of these is common and each one ends in a dispute.

### 7.4 How much to raise, if you raise

| If you need | Raise | Dilution | At |
|---|---|---|---|
| To hire one person and buy equipment | ₹15–25 lakh | 8–12% | ₹1.5–2.5 Cr valuation |
| To build software and run a 100-patient study | ₹40–75 lakh | 12–18% | ₹3–5 Cr valuation |
| To expand to 3 cities | ₹1.5–3 Cr | 18–25% | ₹8–15 Cr valuation |

Raise for a **specific milestone with a date**, never for "runway." "₹40 lakh to get 5 paying sites and a 100-patient outcome study by March" is fundable. "₹40 lakh for 18 months of runway" is not.

### 7.5 Why VC is the wrong money for your goal

You said you want a profitable, self-owned business. Venture capital is structurally incompatible with that, and the incompatibility is arithmetic, not philosophy.

A ₹500 crore fund needs to return ~₹1,500 crore to its own investors. It does that by owning 15–20% of companies that reach ₹1,000 crore+ valuations. Your base case is ₹2–5 crore of annual revenue. To a VC, that is not a modest success — it is a **write-off**, and they will push you toward the growth-at-all-costs path that makes the business fragile and takes control from you.

**What you give up in a typical priced round:** 20–25% now, another 15–20% at the next round, a board seat (or two), liquidation preference (they get paid before you on any sale), anti-dilution protection, veto rights over salary, hiring, and strategy, and an implicit obligation to exit in 7–10 years.

**When VC *is* right for you:** if, at month 24, the engine generalises to a second and third condition and you *choose* to become a venture-scale company. That is a decision to make with evidence, not a decision to make now.

**The honest alternative that works:** ₹1–3 crore from 3–8 customer-investors and angels at a ₹5–10 crore valuation, giving up 15–25% total, keeping the board, keeping control, and building a ₹5–15 crore revenue business. That is a genuinely good outcome, and it is available without a fund.

## 8. The pitch: three decks, three audiences

Most first-time founders build one deck and show it to everyone. That is why most decks fail. A clinic, a grant panel, and an angel are buying three different things.

### 8.1 The five sentences that must be true

Before any slide exists, you must be able to say these without notes:

1. **"We run the follow-up for patients with high blood pressure, for clinics that don't have anyone doing it."**
2. **"It costs the clinic ₹400 per patient, and it fills follow-up slots they were already losing."**
3. **"We've done it for N patients across M clinics; X% completed the 7-day series, and Y% resulted in a treatment decision."**
4. **"We're starting with diagnostic labs because they already visit homes, then moving to polyclinics."**
5. **"In 12 months we'll be at ₹X lakh a month from M sites, funded by customer revenue and a ₹20 lakh grant."**

Every sentence is checkable. If any one is unverifiable, you are not ready to pitch — you are ready to test.

### 8.2 The customer one-pager (not a deck)

Clinics do not read decks. They read one page, or nothing. Layout:

```
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ 7-Day BP Confirmation Programme              │
│ for [Clinic type]                            │
├──────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ The problem   Your patients with a high       │
│               reading leave, and 60–70% never │
│               come back with a home reading.  │
│                                              │
│ What we do    Day 0: enrol + teach proper     │
│               measurement. Day 1–7: reminders │
│               and a call on day 2 and 5.      │
│               Day 8: one-page summary on your │
│               doctor's desk before follow-up. │
│                                              │
│ What you get  Completed series, documented    │
│               readings, a decision-ready      │
│               summary, a monthly report.      │
│                                              │
│ What it costs ₹500/patient, or ₹25,000/month  │
│               for up to 60 patients.          │
│                                              │
│ What we don't Your doctor makes every clinical│
│ do            decision. We do not diagnose,   │
│               advise, or prescribe.           │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

The last box is not a disclaimer — it is a selling point. It tells a busy doctor you understand where your job ends.

### 8.3 The grant deck (10 slides)

For SISFS, BIG, incubators, and state schemes. They fund **innovation and proof of concept**, so lead with the technical problem, not the market size.

| # | Slide | Must prove |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Title + one line | What you do, in words a non-specialist understands |
| 2 | The problem, quantified | A number they can verify, with a citation |
| 3 | Why existing solutions fail | Not "no one does this" — who does, and why it fails |
| 4 | **The innovation** | What is genuinely new: the workflow, the algorithm, the protocol. Grants fund novelty |
| 5 | Technical plan, 18 months | Work packages, milestones, deliverables, month by month |
| 6 | **Budget, line itemised** | Equipment, manpower, consumables, testing, IP. Grant panels read this first |
| 7 | Team | Why you, and who covers the clinical and technical gaps |
| 8 | Regulatory pathway | CDSCO class, timeline, and why the claim you're making is affordable |
| 9 | Impact | Patients reached, cost per patient, public-health relevance |
| 10 | Sustainability | How it survives after the grant: revenue model, next funder, customer commitments |

**The mistake that sinks grant applications:** presenting a service business as innovation. "We make phone calls to patients" is not R&D. "We are building and validating a triage algorithm that identifies which home BP series are adequate for a treatment decision, reducing unnecessary clinic visits by X%" is.

### 8.4 The angel deck (12 slides)

For customer-investors and angels, if you choose to raise (§7.3).

| # | Slide | What it must prove | The number |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Title | Clarity | — |
| 2 | Problem | It's expensive, not just unpleasant | Cost of uncontrolled BP per patient/year |
| 3 | Insight | Something non-obvious you learned by doing | e.g. "60% of flagged patients never return" — your own data |
| 4 | Product | One screenshot, one flow | — |
| 5 | **Traction** | Revenue, not pilots | ₹X/month, N sites, M patients |
| 6 | Unit economics | Per-site and per-patient | ₹/patient, GM%, payback |
| 7 | Market | Bottom-up, not "1.4B people" | Sites × patients × price |
| 8 | Competition | Honest map, including "nobody" and "the clinic does it badly" | — |
| 9 | Business model | Who pays, how often, how it renews | — |
| 10 | Roadmap | 18 months, 4 milestones | — |
| 11 | Team | Why this team, including the clinical gap and how it's covered | — |
| 12 | Ask | Amount, use of funds, milestone it buys | ₹X for Y by Z |

**Slide 5 is the whole pitch.** With ₹1.5 lakh/month of real revenue, everything else can be imperfect. Without it, everything else is a story.

### 8.5 The eight numbers to memorise

You will be asked these in every serious conversation, and hesitating on any one of them costs you the meeting.

1. Patients served to date, and how many completed.
2. Revenue to date, and monthly revenue now.
3. Revenue per patient, and gross margin.
4. Cost to acquire one site, and months to payback.
5. Completion rate, and doctor-action rate.
6. Churn: how many sites renewed, how many didn't.
7. Cash burn per month, and months of runway.
8. The next milestone, its date, and what it unlocks.

If you cannot answer all eight from memory, you are not ready to pitch — you are ready to go and measure them.

### 8.6 Twenty objections, with answers

**About the business**

1. *"This is just a call centre."* — "It's a documented pathway with a measured completion rate. The calls are how it's delivered today; the data and the protocol are what we're building."
2. *"Why won't the clinic do it themselves?"* — "They've had ten years to. The blocker is staff time and nobody owning the follow-up list, not knowledge."
3. *"What stops a competitor copying you?"* — "Nothing, in year one. What's hard to copy is the clinical relationships and the completion-rate playbook, and those compound."
4. *"Why are you the person?"* — "I've run N cohorts, I know exactly where patients drop off, and I have M clinics that will take my call. That's more than a domain expert with no customers."
5. *"You have no medical background."* — "Correct. That's why every clinical decision sits with the clinic and my protocol is signed off by a clinician. It's also why I'm cheaper than hiring a doctor to do logistics."

**About the market**

6. *"Isn't the market tiny?"* — "At ₹500 per patient and 50 patients a month per site, one site is ₹3 lakh a year. Fifty sites is ₹1.5 crore. I don't need a big market, I need fifty sites."
7. *"Why India? Nobody pays for healthcare here."* — "Institutions pay, not consumers. A lab that already sells a ₹1,200 package will pay ₹500 to make it repeatable."
8. *"What happens when Apple does this?"* — "Apple's hypertension feature has 41% sensitivity and tells you to see a doctor. It creates my customer, it doesn't replace me."
9. *"Why now?"* — "Because home BP devices are cheap and ubiquitous, WhatsApp is the default patient channel, and labs are all chasing chronic-disease revenue. None of that was true five years ago."
10. *"Isn't this a feature of an EMR?"* — "EMRs record. They don't call patients. Every EMR vendor in India will tell you follow-up is the customer's problem."

**About money**

11. *"How do you scale without capital?"* — "Each site pays before I deliver. Growth is limited by sales, not cash. That's slower and safer."
12. *"What's your CAC?"* — "₹X per site, mostly my time. Payback in Y months at ₹25,000/month."
13. *"Why not raise a proper round?"* — "Because I'd be selling 25% of a company to build something that doesn't need the money. If the second condition works, I'll raise then."
14. *"What if you can't hire?"* — "I don't need to hire until 8 sites. The first hire is a coordinator, not an engineer."
15. *"What's the exit?"* — "Acquisition by a diagnostics chain or an RPM platform is the realistic path in 5–7 years, at 3–5× revenue. I'm not optimising for it."

**About risk**

16. *"Regulatory risk?"* — "We don't diagnose, so we're not a device. The day we add clinical decision support, we file with CDSCO. That's a deliberate sequencing choice."
17. *"What if completion rates are bad?"* — "They were X% in the first cohort. If they fall below 40%, the model is broken and I'd know within six weeks, not six years."
18. *"What if the pilot clinic churns?"* — "Then I learn why before I have twenty of them. That's the point of doing this with one site."
19. *"What if a big lab builds it in-house?"* — "They might. Then I sell to the other nine chains, or I become the vendor they buy. Either is fine."
20. *"What do you do if this fails?"* — "I'll have two years of clinical operations experience, a network of 50 clinicians, and a documented playbook. That's a job offer at any healthtech company, or a second attempt with better odds."

### 8.7 The demo problem: what to show with no product

You will be asked to "show the product" before you have one. Three honest substitutes, in order of power:

1. **The artifact.** A completed one-page summary from a real (consented) patient, with the numbers filled in. This is the product.
2. **The process video.** A 90-second screen recording of you doing the work: the sheet, the reminder, the summary generated in 20 seconds.
3. **The before/after.** A folder of 30 unstructured WhatsApp messages versus one page. The contrast sells itself.

**Never** show a Figma mockup of an app you haven't built. It invites questions about the app instead of the outcome, and it makes you look like every other founder who has a design and no customers.

## 9. Cap table and control, if you take money

Skip this chapter if you never raise. Read it before you sign anything if you do — because the mistakes here are permanent.

### 9.1 The dilution arithmetic, worked

Start: you own 100%. Then, in sequence:

| Event | Your % | Outside % | What it cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Advisor grant (0.5%, 2-yr vest) | 99.5% | 0.5% | A clinician's name and 2 hrs/month |
| ESOP pool created (10%, pre-round) | 89.5% | 10.5% | Future hires — this dilutes **you**, not investors |
| Angel round ₹40L at ₹4Cr post-money | 80.6% | 19.4% | ~9% for ₹40L |
| Next round ₹2Cr at ₹16Cr post | 70.5% | 29.5% | ~10% more |
| ESOP top-up (5%) | 67.0% | 33.0% | — |

After two rounds you own about two-thirds — and you still control the company, because control is not ownership.

**The ESOP trap:** investors will ask for an ESOP pool *before* the round, sized as a percentage of post-money. A 10% pool created pre-money costs you 10%; created post-money it costs you ~8.5%. On a ₹4 crore valuation that is ₹60 lakh of your personal value. Negotiate it explicitly.

### 9.2 What control actually means in an Indian Pvt Ltd

| Threshold | What it gives you | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **>50%** | Ordinary resolutions: appoint directors, approve most business decisions | Day-to-day control |
| **≥75%** | Special resolutions alone: change articles, issue new shares, mergers, winding up | The real control threshold |
| **26%** | Blocking minority: you can block special resolutions | The floor you never want to cross |
| **Board seat** | Operational control regardless of shares | Often more important than equity |

**Practical rule:** do not go below 51% before year 5, and do not go below 26% ever, unless the company is worth more to you as a minority stake than as a business.

### 9.3 The five terms that matter more than valuation

First-time founders negotiate valuation and ignore these. Each one can cost you the company.

1. **Liquidation preference.** "1× non-participating" means investors get their money back first, then share the remainder. "2× participating" means they take double their money *and* then share — in a ₹6 crore sale after a ₹4 crore round, you could get almost nothing. Never accept participating preference above 1×.
2. **Anti-dilution.** "Full ratchet" re-prices their shares if you raise at a lower valuation, devastating you in a down round. Accept only broad-based weighted average, or nothing.
3. **Board composition.** A 3-person board (you, investor, independent) is workable. A 5-person board where investors hold 2 plus the independent is control in all but name.
4. **Reserved matters.** The list of things needing investor consent. Salary, hiring, and spending above a threshold should not be on it. Keep it to share issuance, debt above a limit, sale of the company, and changes to the business line.
5. **Vesting on your own shares.** Investors may ask for founder vesting. Accept 4 years with a 1-year cliff **with credit for time already served**, not from scratch.

### 9.4 What a good small round looks like for you

```
Instrument:        CCPS (or CCD if simpler)
Amount:            ₹25–50 lakh
Valuation:         ₹2.5–4 crore post-money
Dilution:          10–16%
Investors:         3–6 customer-investors and angels, none above 8%
Board:             You + 1 investor observer (no vote)
Liquidation:       1× non-participating
Anti-dilution:     Broad-based weighted average
Reserved matters:  Share issuance, debt >₹50L, sale, business change
ESOP:              8%, created post-money
Pro-rata:          Yes, standard
```

This structure keeps you in control, keeps the cap table clean, and is achievable at ₹30–50 lakh ARR without a fund.

### 10.1 The year at a glance

| Month | Focus | Revenue target | Key output |

| **2** | One-pager built; 10 more conversations | ₹0 | 3 serious prospects |
| **3** | First paid pilot signed and started | ₹15K | First rupee |
| **4** | Deliver 30 patients; measure 4 metrics | ₹15K | First case study |
| **5** | Second and third pilots; raise price to ₹25K | ₹30K | Repeatability signal |
| **6** | **Gate: continue or switch to Route 1** | ₹30–50K | Honest decision |
| **7** | Convert one pilot to monthly retainer | ₹50K | Recurring revenue |
| **8** | Add a coordinator (part-time, ₹15–20K) | ₹75K | You stop being the bottleneck |

| **12** | 5 sites, 300 patients cumulative | ₹1.5–2L | Grant decision pending; ₹18–24L ARR run-rate |

**What "success at month 12" looks like:** 4–6 paying sites, ₹1.5–2 lakh monthly revenue, 55%+ completion, one clinician advisor, one grant application submitted, and a decision about whether to hire.

**What "failure at month 12" looks like:** 20 conversations, zero paid pilots, a nice website. Recognise it early — in month 6, not month 12.

### 10.2 The weekly scorecard (six numbers, every Friday)

| Metric | Why it matters | Red flag |
|---|---|---|
| Conversations held (not emails sent) | Leading indicator of everything | <5/week |
| Proposals sent | Conversion of interest to ask | 0 for 3 weeks |
| Paid pilots/retainers active | Actual business | Flat for 6 weeks |
| **Series completion rate** | The core operational number | <40% |
| Doctor action rate | Whether the output is useful | <30% |
| Cash in bank ÷ monthly burn | Survival | <3 months |

Print it. Fill it by hand. A dashboard you ignore is worse than a notebook you don't.

### 10.3 Personal runway and the quit rule

With ₹0 invested, your real capital is **time and attention**, and both are consumed by whatever pays your rent.

**Define these three numbers this week:**

1. **Personal monthly burn** (rent, food, transport, obligations). Write the actual number, not a hopeful one.
2. **Months of savings.** Divide savings by burn. If it's under 6, do not quit your job.
3. **The revenue threshold** at which the business covers your burn plus its own costs.

**The quit rule:** quit your job when **the business has covered your personal burn for 3 consecutive months**, or when you have **12 months of savings plus a signed ₹1 lakh/month contract**. Not when you feel confident. Not when the deck is done.

The most common first-time-founder failure in India is not a bad idea. It is quitting at month 2 with 5 months of savings, running out at month 7 with no revenue, and taking a job at month 8 with the idea dead. The second most common is never quitting at all. The rule above is how you avoid both.

## 11. Ten failure modes, and the kill criteria

### 11.1 Failure modes specific to this business

| # | Failure mode | Early warning | Fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Pilot purgatory** — endless free pilots, no paid contract | 3 "pilots" and ₹0 collected | Charge for the next one or stop |
| 2 | **Founder-as-bottleneck** — you deliver every patient yourself | Revenue plateaus at ₹1.5L | Hire a coordinator before you think you can afford one |
| 3 | **Scope creep into clinical** — a clinic asks you to "just advise" | You start interpreting readings | Written protocol; refuse, and say why |
| 4 | **Single-customer dependency** | >60% revenue from one site | Cap any customer at 40% |
| 5 | **The chain that never signs** — 6 months of "very interested" at a big lab | No procurement step after 3 meetings | Ask for the purchase order date or walk |
| 6 | **Building instead of selling** | 6 weeks on a product with no customer waiting | Ship nothing until a customer prepays |
| 7 | **Price anchoring low** | You quoted ₹5,000 once and everyone heard | Never discount; requote new customers higher |
| 8 | **Data-protection incident** | Patient data on your personal phone | Move to a business number and a controlled sheet now |
| 9 | **Grant chasing** — 9 months of applications, no revenue | Applications > customers | One application per quarter, maximum |
| 10 | **Ego pivot** — abandoning a working service for a shinier product | Revenue flat while you "reposition" | Revenue is the only vote that counts |

### 11.2 Kill criteria (write these down and honour them)

| Checkpoint | Kill if |
|---|---|
| **Month 6** | Fewer than 2 paid pilots, despite 30+ conversations |
| **Month 9** | Completion rate below 40% after one process redesign |
| **Month 12** | Revenue below ₹75,000/month, or no renewal from any site |
| **Month 18** | Fewer than 4 sites, or gross margin below 50% |
| **Month 24** | Not profitable or clearly on a path to it within 6 months |

**What "kill" means:** stop spending on the business, return remaining capital if any, and move to Route 1 with the assets you built — relationships, a playbook, and a documented understanding of a real workflow. That is not a wasted two years. It is a funded education, and it is more than most founders have when they start their second company.

### 11.3 The one thing to remember from this document

> **You are not building a product. You are buying evidence, one paid pilot at a time, with someone else's clinical judgement and your own operational discipline.**

Everything else — the deck, the grant, the software, the cap table — exists to support that sentence.

## Appendix B — Beginner glossary

| Term | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| **ARR / MRR** | Annual / monthly recurring revenue from subscriptions or retainers. Excludes one-off pilots |
| **Gross margin** | Revenue minus direct delivery cost, as a %. Your time counts if it's paid |
| **CAC** | What it costs to win one customer, including your time at a notional rate |
| **LTV** | Total gross profit from one customer over their life. Not revenue |
| **Payback** | Months of revenue to recover CAC |
| **Runway** | Cash ÷ monthly burn |
| **Burn** | Monthly cash spent, net of revenue |
| **Cap table** | Who owns what % of the company |
| **Dilution** | Your % falling when new shares are issued |
| **Vesting / cliff** | Earning equity over time; a cliff is the minimum period before any vests |
| **ESOP** | Option pool reserved for employees |
| **CCPS / CCD** | Compulsorily convertible preference shares / debentures — Indian investment instruments that must convert to equity |
| **SAFE / convertible note** | A loan-like instrument that converts to equity later at a discount or cap |
| **Liquidation preference** | Investors get paid first on a sale, before founders |
| **Anti-dilution** | Protection for investors if you raise at a lower valuation |
| **Tag-along / drag-along** | Rights to join a sale / to force you into one |
| **DPIIT** | The government department that recognises startups (free, online) |
| **SaMD** | Software as a Medical Device — regulated |
| **CDSCO** | India's drug and device regulator |
| **DPDP** | India's data protection law (2023 Act, 2025 Rules) |
| **IEC** | Ethics committee that approves research on humans |
| **ABPM** | 24-hour ambulatory BP monitoring, cuff-based |
| **OPD / IPD** | Outpatient department / inpatient department |
| **LTV/CAC** | Health ratio; above 3 is good, below 1 is fatal |

## Appendix C — Five books, not fifty

1. **The Mom Test** — Rob Fitzpatrick. How to talk to customers without fooling yourself. Read this first; it will save you six months.
2. **Disciplined Entrepreneurship** — Bill Aulet. 24 steps, in order, for a first-time founder. The structure most startup advice lacks.
3. **Venture Deals** — Brad Feld & Jason Mendelson. Read before any term sheet, even if you never raise.
4. **The Hard Thing About Hard Things** — Ben Horowitz. What it actually feels like when it's going badly.
5. **Crossing the Chasm** — Geoffrey Moore. Why "everyone loves it" doesn't turn into revenue.

Skip the rest until you have revenue. Most startup books are written for companies that already raised money.

## Appendix E — The 30-day checklist

Print this. Tick it. Nothing else matters until it's done.

**Week 1**
- [ ] Write your personal monthly burn number, honestly
- [ ] Write your months of savings
- [ ] Decide: employed, part-time, or full-time for the next 90 days
- [ ] List 40 target institutions with a named contact each
- [ ] Open a separate bank account for the business

**Week 2**
- [ ] Hold 8 conversations using the five questions (§3.2)
- [ ] Log every answer verbatim in the sheet
- [ ] Identify the phrase "we don't have anyone for that" — count how often it appears
- [ ] Apply to 5 jobs as income insurance (Route 1)

**Week 3**
- [ ] Build the two-page one-pager and the sample summary
- [ ] Print 20 copies
- [ ] Hold 8 more conversations
- [ ] Ask 3 people for a paid pilot at ₹15,000

**Week 4**
- [ ] Sign one pilot, or record why all three said no
- [ ] Set up WhatsApp Business and a consent-approved intake message
- [ ] Take professional indemnity insurance quote
- [ ] Book the day-90 review with yourself, in writing

**By day 30 you should have:** 16+ conversations, a documented problem statement in customers' own words, one pilot signed or three documented rejections, and a clear answer to whether you can continue.
