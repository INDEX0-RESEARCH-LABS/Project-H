## 0. The vision, in one page

### 0.1 What we are building

> **We own the seven days between a high blood-pressure reading and a doctor's decision.**

Half the world's hypertensive adults do not know they have it. The ones who do find out are told to "check it at home" and then vanish. Not because they are careless — because nobody's job is to chase them, and nobody is paid to know whether the readings ever came back.

We are that job. A measured, auditable, seven-day confirmation pathway: enrolment, a clinician-written protocol, reminders, calls to the people who stop, and one page on the doctor's desk before the follow-up. The doctor decides. We make sure there is something to decide about.

### 0.2 What that becomes

| Horizon | What exists | What it is worth |
|---|---|---|
| **Year 1** | One working pathway in one city, paid for by customers | ₹18–24 lakh revenue. Proof that people complete it |
| **Year 3** | 30–40 sites, a product, a validated adequacy score | ₹1.0–1.4 crore revenue. Profitable. No outside control |
| **Year 5** | 100 sites, a licence line, a second condition being tested | ₹3.3–3.9 crore revenue, ₹70–90 lakh EBITDA |
| **Year 7** | 180 sites plus licensed infrastructure, or a second condition at scale | ₹6.5–7.5 crore revenue, ₹1.4–1.7 crore EBITDA |

### 0.3 The two futures, stated plainly

**Future A — the good one, and the likely one.** ₹3–7 crore of revenue, 15–30 people, 20–25% EBITDA, you own ~80%, no investor can remove you, and you draw ₹60–90 lakh a year by year 6 between salary and distributions. This is not a unicorn. It is a durable, boring, profitable healthcare business that employs people and changes what happens to a few hundred thousand patients. **This is the plan.**

**Future B — the one you should also plan for.** It stops at 25–30 sites and ₹1.2 crore of revenue. Six people. ₹25–30 lakh of EBITDA. You earn ₹20–25 lakh a year, the business is stable, and it never becomes famous. **This is not a failure.** It is a good outcome that most founders would take, and it happens if growth stalls for any of the ordinary reasons.

There is a Future C — venture scale — which requires the second condition to generalise and a different kind of company. It is available at month 24 if the evidence supports it. It is not the plan, and chasing it before the evidence exists is how Future A dies.

### 0.4 The five sentences

Every document, every meeting, every hire starts from these. They must stay true or the plan is wrong.

1. **We run the follow-up for patients with a high blood pressure reading, for clinics that have nobody doing it.**
2. **We cost the clinic ₹400–500 per patient and fill follow-up slots they were already losing.**
3. **The doctor makes every clinical decision. We own the chasing, the documentation, and the measurement.**
4. **We start with diagnostic labs because they already send people into homes, then move to polyclinics.**
5. **We are built to be profitable and self-owned, not to be sold.**

### 0.5 Why this can exist at all

Three things became true in the last five years, and none of them were true before:

| Enabler | Why it matters now |
|---|---|
| Home BP devices are cheap and everywhere | Measurement no longer requires a clinic visit |
| WhatsApp is the default patient channel in India | Delivery costs near zero; no app to build, no download to lose |
| Labs and clinics are chasing chronic-disease revenue | There is a commercial motive to pay for follow-up, not just a clinical one |

Detection is meanwhile becoming free: consumer devices now flag hypertension and refer to a clinician [MR S13]. That does not threaten this business — **it manufactures the demand.** Every notification creates a patient who needs confirmation. The notification stops at the clinic door; we are what happens after it.

### 0.6 The arc — three altitudes, one company

The vision is bigger than the product. That is deliberate, not a contradiction: investors fund a large market entered through a narrow wedge; customers buy a narrow product.

| Altitude | The claim | True by | Who hears it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Product** | A 7-day BP confirmation pathway for labs and clinics | Now (designed) | Lab owner, clinic manager |
| **Business** | The follow-through layer for chronic disease in Indian primary care | Year 3 | Grant panel, customer-investor |
| **Vision** | **Confirmation infrastructure** — every home measurement becomes actionable at the clinic | Year 5+, only if the machinery proves reusable | Venture investor, strategic partner |

**The sentence, used verbatim:**

> "We're building the confirmation layer for Indian healthcare. Today that means seven days of blood pressure follow-up for labs, because that's the cheapest place to prove the machinery works. The machinery — chase the patient, verify the data is good enough, summarise it, route it to a decision — is what every home measurement in this country is missing."

**Four rules that keep the arc honest:**

1. **Altitude follows the room.** Product claims with customers. The arc with capital. Never the reverse — a lab owner does not care about infrastructure, and an investor does not fund a follow-up service.
2. **The arc is a claim about reuse, not scope.** Nothing in it requires building a second condition this year. It requires the BP pathway to be built so 70% of it transfers.
3. **Never list conditions in an idea-stage deck.** "BP, diabetes, thyroid, post-surgical" reads as unfocused. "Confirmation infrastructure" reads as big. Same company, different sentence.
4. **The arc has a falsifier.** If at year 3 the second condition's pathway is not at least 70% reusable, the vision was wrong and the company is a BP service business — which is still a good business. Say that out loud in the room. It is the difference between a vision and a slogan.

**What this costs today:** nothing. The work is naming the reusable asset (adequacy engine, reminder engine, summary format, data model) and logging every cohort from patient one so the asset accumulates.

## 1. What we refuse to do

A vision is defined more by its exclusions than its ambitions. These are permanent unless the evidence changes them, and the evidence would have to be overwhelming.

| We will never | Why |
|---|---|
| **Diagnose, prescribe, or advise on treatment** | It is practising medicine without registration, and it destroys the liability position that makes us usable by a clinic |
| **Sell or monetise patient data** | One deal and no clinic trusts us again. It is also a DPDP problem we do not need [MR S24] |
| **Build a consumer app** | Patients will not download software for seven days. WhatsApp is the app |
| **Build hardware** | ₹5–30 crore and 24 months before revenue [MR §8.2]. It is a different company with different investors |
| **Chase a big lab chain in year one** | Six months of "very interested", no purchase order, no learning. Small sites decide in one meeting |
| **Take venture capital before month 24** | It buys growth we cannot absorb and sells control we need for a business this size |
| **Make a clinical claim without a study** | One overclaim ends the clinical relationships that are the whole asset |
| **Run three cities at once** | Geographic spread before repeatability is how service businesses die |
| **Discount the price** | Reduce the scope instead. A discount is a permanent reference point |
| **Say "AI" when there is no model** | It invites technical diligence we would fail, and it is not what the customer is buying |

**The one thing that is not negotiable at any stage:** the clinician owns every clinical decision. If a future feature blurs that line, the feature waits until we can afford the regulation — or it does not ship.

## 2. The thesis

### 2.1 In one sentence

> **In hypertension, the bottleneck is not detection or treatment — it is the confirmation step in between, and whoever owns that step owns the patient relationship.**

### 2.2 The three claims it rests on

**Claim 1 — Detection is commoditising, confirmation is not.**

Consumer hypertension notification is FDA-cleared and has 41.2% sensitivity in undiagnosed hypertensives (n=2,229) [MR S13, S14]. It catches about four in ten true cases and refers the rest to a clinician. A device "all clear" misses six in ten. Detection will be free on every wrist within three years. **What does not commoditise is a human-run pathway that turns a signal into a decision**, because it depends on local clinical relationships and operational discipline, not on a sensor.

**Claim 2 — The failure is workflow, not technology.**

In no country does wearable data reach clinical workflow above 6% of physicians (n=2,222, six countries) [MR S25]. Every player has solved the measurement and nobody has solved the handover. That gap is where the value sits, and it is a services problem before it is a software problem.

**Claim 3 — The buyer is an institution, not a consumer.**

India's smartwatch market is 28.9 million units at a $26.5 ASP, shrinking 17.6% [MR S10]. Consumer willingness to pay is thin and churn is brutal. Meanwhile a lab that already sells a chronic-disease package has a budget, a repeat need, and a reason to pay for follow-up. **Sell to the institution; the patient pays nothing.**

### 2.3 What would falsify the thesis

State this now, so it cannot be rationalised later.

| If this is true | Then the thesis is wrong | Where we would know |
|---|---|---|
| Fewer than 40% of enrolled patients complete a 7-day series after one redesign | The confirmation step is not deliverable by a third party | First pilot, week 6 |
| Doctors ignore the summary (documented action <30%) | The output is decorative and the clinic will not renew | First pilot, month 3 |
| No clinic will pay ₹15,000 for 30 patients after 20 conversations | The pain is not expensive enough to buy | Month 4 |
| Sites renew at under 50% | It is a project business, not a service business | Month 9–12 |
| A lab chain or EMR vendor bundles it free and clinics accept | The wedge is not defensible | Year 2–3 |

**The honest position:** the first three falsifiers are untested. Everything in this document is a plan to test them cheaply, in that order.

## 3. The product, in five stages

The product is not an app. It is a pathway that gets progressively less manual. Each stage has a trigger; no stage starts before its trigger.

### 3.1 Stage 0 — Manual concierge (now to month 6)

| | |
|---|---|
| **What it is** | You, a phone, a Google Sheet, a printed card, a Word template |
| **Delivered by** | You, entirely |
| **Cash cost** | Under ₹25,000 |
| **Price** | ₹15,000 / 30 patients |
| **Trigger to leave** | 10 paying cohorts delivered, and you are turning work away |

**What you are actually buying at this stage:** the answer to "where do patients drop off", measured, in a real clinic, with real staff. That knowledge is the product. The service is the vehicle.

### 3.2 Stage 1 — Templated (month 4–9)

| | |
|---|---|
| **What changes** | Validated intake sheet, mail-merged summaries, a saved reminder library, a fixed drop-off view |
| **Delivered by** | You plus one part-time coordinator |
| **Cash cost** | ₹0–1,500/month in tools |
| **Price** | ₹25,000–40,000/month retainer, 50–80 patients |
| **Trigger to leave** | 3 paying sites, and delivery still surprises you weekly |

### 3.3 Stage 2 — Productised platform (month 9–18)

| | |
|---|---|
| **What changes** | Intake form, automated reminder sequence, summary generation, multi-site dashboard, audit log |
| **Delivered by** | Coordinator plus you on exceptions |
| **Cash cost** | ₹4.5 lakh build (in the ₹25 lakh plan) |
| **Price** | ₹350–500 per patient at volume |
| **Trigger to leave** | 8–10 paying sites and a repeatable sales motion |

**The rule:** never encode a process that still surprises you weekly. You will encode the wrong thing, and it will cost more to unlearn than to have done by hand.

### 3.4 Stage 3 — The validated instrument (month 12–18)

This is where the company stops being a service and starts owning something.

| | |
|---|---|
| **What it is** | An **adequacy score**: given a 7-day series with missing readings, artefacts, and variable timing, return *sufficient* / *repeat* / *needs clinic*, with a reason code a clinician can audit |
| **Why it matters** | Nobody has validated this. Clinicians eyeball it today. It is the difference between "we chase patients" and "we can tell you whether what came back is usable" |
| **How it is built** | Rules-based score plus a comparison statistical model, validated against independent clinical adjudication (see [P2] for the study design and budget) |
| **Regulatory position** | Decision *support* for a clinician, not autonomous diagnosis. A CDSCO classification opinion is part of the study [MR S23] |
| **Trigger to leave** | Validation complete, and a customer asks to license it |

**Why this is the moat candidate and not the software:** reminders and dashboards are copyable in a quarter. A validated decision instrument with a published method, a reason code, and a regulatory opinion takes two years and a study. That is the only thing worth defending.

### 3.5 Stage 4 — Licensed infrastructure or a second condition (year 3–5)

Two doors, and you choose one with evidence:

| Door | What it means | Signal to take it |
|---|---|---|
| **Licence** | Sell the adequacy score and the pathway to RPM platforms, lab chains, and hospital groups | Three customers ask for it unprompted |
| **Second condition** | Apply the same confirmation logic to diabetes (HbA1c follow-up), thyroid, or post-discharge monitoring | Confirmation works at 100 sites and the second condition's pathway is 70% identical |

**Do not do both in the same year.** Each is a company-building exercise, and doing two at once is how the first one stalls.

### 3.6 What never changes across all five stages

| Invariant | Why |
|---|---|
| The clinician decides | It is the legal boundary and the sales argument |
| Completion is measured and reported per site | It is the only honest proof of value |
| The patient pays nothing | It keeps the buyer single and the economics clean |
| We never sell data | It protects the only asset that compounds |
| One city before two | Repeatability before geography |

## 4. The business model

### 4.1 The pricing ladder

| Stage | Structure | Price | Who approves it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pilot | Fixed cohort | ₹15,000 / 30 patients | One person, one meeting |
| Repeat | Monthly retainer | ₹25,000–40,000 / 50–80 patients | Clinic manager |
| Multi-site | Per patient, volume-tiered | ₹350–500 | Procurement |
| Chain | Annual contract, SLA | ₹4–12 lakh / year | Committee |
| Licence | Annual, per-site or per-patient | TBD | Committee |

**The jump that kills first-time founders:** staying on pilots because pilots are easy to say yes to. Ask for the retainer the moment the case study lands, not three months later.

### 4.3 The services plateau — and how it is crossed

A services business plateaus where the founder's calendar ends. Ours will plateau around 8–10 sites, roughly ₹2.5–3 lakh a month, unless three things change in order:

| Constraint | Fix | When |
|---|---|---|
| Delivery is you | Coordinator trained on a written playbook | Month 7–8 |
| Quality varies by site | Clinical operations lead; standard protocol training | Month 12–15 |
| Sales is you | First sales hire, commission-heavy | Month 18–24 |

**The plateau is not a failure; it is a signal.** Hitting it means the model works and the constraint has moved from demand to capacity — a much better problem than the reverse.

### 4.4 What we never monetise

| Never | Why |
|---|---|
| Patient data, in any form | Trust, DPDP, and it is not a real business at our scale |
| Advertising or sponsored content to patients | Corrupts the clinical pathway |
| Device sales | Low margin, high support, different company |
| "Consulting" for clinics on how to do it themselves | Cannibalises the only product, and they will not pay twice |
| A free tier to acquire users | There are no users. There are sites. Sites pay |

## 5. The market, bottom-up

### 5.1 India

Do not quote a rupee market size. Build it.

| Layer | Number | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Indian adults with hypertension | 210.2 million | [MR S12] |
| Uncontrolled | 173.9 million | [MR S12] |
| Standalone pathology labs (urban) | ~12,000 | [T] estimate |
| Urban polyclinics (2–5 doctors) | ~50,000 | [T] estimate |
| Our year-5 target | 100–300 sites | [T] |
| Revenue per site per year | ₹3.6 lakh | [T] |
| **Revenue at 100 sites** | **₹3.6 crore** | [T] |
| Revenue at 300 sites | ₹10.8 crore | [T] |

**The line that matters in a meeting:** *"I need one hundred sites. This city has four thousand."* A clinic owner understands that arithmetic instantly; a market-size slide makes them suspicious.

### 5.2 The US second act, and why it waits

US remote monitoring reimburses roughly $102–160 per patient-month across CPT 99453/99454/99457/99458 [MR S26]. That is 100–200× the Indian per-patient price. It is a real second act.

It also requires: a US entity, HIPAA compliance, a US clinical champion, malpractice cover, and roughly $450K–1.6M to a first clearance if we ever touch a regulated claim [MR §8]. **You cannot fund that from Indian service revenue, and you should not try before year 3.**

The sequencing is not patriotism. It is that India is where you can afford to be wrong: 30 patients cost ₹15,000 to test, not $50,000. The evidence you generate in India is what makes a US partner or payer conversation possible later, and it is the only asset that transfers.

### 5.3 What we deliberately do not count

| Excluded | Why |
|---|---|
| Consumer wearable owners as a market | They are not the buyer, and the churn is brutal |
| "1.4 billion hypertensives" as a TAM | True, useless, and it marks you as unserious |
| Insurance reimbursement in India | Not reliable at our scale in the next three years |
| Government contracts | Long cycles, payment delays that would kill a ₹0-capital company |
| Corporate wellness | Attractive, but a 9–12 month sales cycle. Year 3, not year 1 |

## 6. Competition and the moat

### 6.1 The real competitor

| Who | What they do | Why they don't solve it |
|---|---|---|
| **The clinic's own staff** | Say "check it at home" | No owner, no list, no follow-up, no measurement. **This is 90% of the market** |
| Consumer wearables | Detect and notify | 41.2% sensitivity; they hand the problem to the clinic [MR S13] |
| EMR / clinic software | Record readings | Record; do not chase patients |
| RPM platforms | Device plus dashboard, sold to hospitals | Built for US billing; no India last-mile, no human follow-up |
| Diagnostic labs | Sell BP inside a package | One-off measurement, no 7-day confirmation, no titration loop |
| Hospital-at-home startups | Clinical care at home | Different product, different buyer, much higher cost per patient |

**Naming the clinic's own staff as the competitor is the most important strategic sentence in this document.** It tells you what you are actually selling — a job nobody wants to do — and it tells an investor you are not deluded about an empty field.

### 6.2 The moat, honestly ranked

| Layer | Strength | Time to copy |
|---|---|---|
| **Clinical relationships** | Strongest. Fifty lab owners who take your call | Years, and only by walking in |
| **Completion-rate playbook** | Strong. Knowing exactly which reminder at which hour moves which patient | 12–18 months of iteration, and only with real cohorts |
| **Validated adequacy score** | Strong, if it validates | 2 years plus a study |
| **Distribution density in one city** | Medium. Being the default answer when a lab asks | 2 years |
| **Software** | Weak. Copyable in a quarter | 3 months |
| **Price** | None. Never compete here | — |

**The uncomfortable truth:** in year one you have no moat at all. What you have is speed and a willingness to do unglamorous work. The moat is built by the data you accumulate while competitors are still deciding whether the market is big enough.

### 6.3 What erodes the moat

| Threat | Likelihood | Response |
|---|---|---|
| A lab chain builds it in-house | Medium | They become a customer or an acquirer; sell to the other nine chains meanwhile |
| An EMR vendor bundles follow-up | Medium | Compete on completion rate, which EMR vendors do not measure |
| An RPM platform enters India with a US-funded product | Medium | Partner or license rather than fight; they need last-mile, you have it |
| A hospital group does it for its own network | High, and fine | That is one customer segment, not the market |
| Price collapse from a well-funded entrant | Low-medium | Never discount; move up-market to chains and licences |

## 7. The seven-year arc

### 7.1 Base case (the plan)

| Year | Sites | Revenue (realised) | Exit run-rate | Team | Capital in | Profitability |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | 5–8 | ₹18–24 L | ₹1.5–2.4 L/mo | 1.5 | ₹0 + customer prepayments | Not yet |
| **2** | 15–20 | ₹45–65 L | ₹4.5–6.0 L/mo | 4 | ₹20 L grant + ₹25 L customer-investor | Breakeven Q3 |
| **3** | 30–40 | ₹1.0–1.4 Cr | ₹9–12 L/mo | 8–10 | ₹0 (self-funded) | 15–20% EBITDA |
| **4** | 60–70 | ₹1.8–2.4 Cr | ₹18–21 L/mo | 14 | ₹0, possibly a small round | 18–22% EBITDA |
| **5** | 100 | ₹3.3–3.9 Cr | ₹30 L/mo | 18–20 | ₹0 | 20–25% EBITDA |
| **6** | 140 + licence | ₹4.5–5.4 Cr | ₹44 L/mo | 25 | ₹0 | 20–25% EBITDA |
| **7** | 180 + licence | ₹6.5–7.5 Cr | ₹59 L/mo | 30 | ₹0 | 22–25% EBITDA |

**Check the arithmetic:** 100 sites × ₹30,000/month × 12 = ₹3.6 crore run-rate. Realised revenue is lower than run-rate in a growth year because sites are added through the year; the licence line adds ₹0.3–1.0 crore from year 5. Every number here follows from site count and price, not from a growth-rate assumption.

### 7.2 Slow case (plan for it)

| Year | Sites | Revenue | Team | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3–5 | ₹10–15 L | 1 | Works, but slowly |
| 2 | 8–10 | ₹30–38 L | 2 | One city, one coordinator |
| 3 | 15–20 | ₹50–70 L | 4 | Stable, profitable, unremarkable |
| 5 | 25–30 | ₹0.95–1.1 Cr | 6 | ₹22–28 L EBITDA; founder draws ₹20–25 L |
| 7 | 30–35 | ₹1.15–1.3 Cr | 7 | A good business. Not a story |

**The slow case is not a failure.** It is roughly a 25% cash yield on effort, with control, in a market that will still be there. Most founders would take it.

### 7.3 Accelerated case (only with evidence)

Requires the second condition to generalise *and* a validated instrument. Then:

| Year | Sites | Revenue | Team | Capital |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3 | 50 | ₹1.6–2.0 Cr | 12 | ₹1–2 Cr round |
| 5 | 150 + licence | ₹8–9 Cr | 35 | ₹5–10 Cr round |
| 7 | 250 + licence | ₹17–20 Cr | 70 | Optional |

**This case needs a different company**, a different investor, and a founder who wants it. It is available at month 24. **Do not decide now.** Decide with five sites' worth of data and a validated instrument in hand.

**Note the shape of it:** the accelerated case is carried by licence revenue, not by site count. Two hundred and fifty sites would require ~70 staff and a national operation; ₹8 crore of licence revenue requires fifteen. If the instrument validates, the sensible accelerated path is fewer sites and more licences — which is also the version a venture investor will find harder to fund, because it is capital-light. That tension is real and belongs in the fork conversation, not in a deck.

### 7.4 The month-24 fork

Three questions, answered with data, not ambition:

| Question | If yes | If no |
|---|---|---|
| Does the pathway work at 10 sites with a coordinator, without you delivering? | Keep going | Fix the process before growing |
| Does the cohort data support validating the adequacy score? | Apply for the study grant | Stay a service business; it is still good |
| Do you *want* 30 people and a board, or ₹1.5 crore a year and control? | Take the accelerated path | Take the base path, deliberately |

**Write the answer down on the day you answer it.** The failure mode is answering it differently every month depending on who you had coffee with.

| # | Role | Month | Cost | Trigger | What breaks without them |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Coordinator | 7–8 | ₹15–20 K | 3 sites, turning work away | You are the bottleneck; revenue caps at ₹1.5 L |

| 3 | Engineer / product (contract first) | 15–18 | ₹60–90 K | 8–10 sites, manual work is the constraint | You cannot serve 30 sites on a spreadsheet |
| 4 | Sales (commission-heavy) | 18–24 | ₹30 K + 10% | 15 sites, and you are the only seller | Growth stays linear in your calendar |
| 5 | Ops analyst | 24–30 | ₹35–45 K | 30 sites | Reporting becomes a weekend job |

### 8.2 Hires that kill companies at this stage

| Hire | Why it kills | What to do instead |
|---|---|---|
| Marketing manager | No repeatable product to market; they will spend on ads for a B2B service | You sell |
| Business development / partnerships | Six months of meetings, no revenue, and they cannot close without a case study | You sell |
| Full-time engineer before a product spec | ₹6 L/month burn building the wrong thing | Contract, milestone-based |
| CFO / finance lead | Nothing to control yet | A CA on retainer, ₹20 K/month |
| "Growth hacker" | There are 40 buyers in your city, not 40,000 users | A list and a phone |
| Anyone senior from a big company | They will want a team, a budget, and a title you cannot support | Hire operators, not executives |

### 8.3 Decision rights you never give away

Even with investors, these stay with the founder. Put them in the shareholders' agreement.

| Right | Why |
|---|---|
| Clinical boundary (no diagnosis, no advice) | It is the licence to operate. A board majority should never be able to change it |
| Pricing | Discount pressure from an investor who is also a customer is how the model dies |
| Who we sell to (and who we refuse) | Reputation is the asset |
| Hiring below ₹50 K/month | Speed matters more than consensus at this size |
| The decision to stay small | The entire point of the outcome you chose |

### 8.4 Clinical governance

| Role | Who | What they own |
|---|---|---|
| **Clinical advisor** | A physician, 2 hrs/month, 0.5% vesting over 24 months [P3] | Protocol sign-off, monthly call, named reference |
| **Treating clinician (per site)** | The customer's doctor | Every clinical decision, and the site's consent process |
| **Adjudication panel** | Two independent clinicians plus a third for disagreements | Only for the validation study [P2] |
| **You** | Founder | The pathway, the data pipeline, the measurement, the commercial relationship |

**The governance rule:** your judgement never enters a measurement. You operate the protocol; a clinician writes it; another clinician judges whether the output was adequate. That separation is what makes the evidence credible and what keeps you out of regulation.

## 9. Capital stack and dilution

### 9.1 The seven-year stack

| Source | Amount | When | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Customer prepayments | ₹15 K–2 L | Month 1 onward | Free |
| Grant (SISFS / BIG) | ₹20–50 L | Month 9–18 | Free, restricted, slow [P2] |
| Customer-investor | ₹25–50 L | Month 12–24 | 10–16% equity [P4] |

| Working capital / RBF | ₹10–25 L | Month 24+ | 14–18% interest |
| Venture round | ₹2–10 Cr | **Only if the fork says so** | 20–30% and control |

**Total outside equity in the base case: ₹25–50 lakh, or 10–16%.** That is the entire dilution of a ₹7 crore revenue business. It is possible because the business funds itself from month 12.

### 9.2 Dilution across the three cases

| Event | Base | Slow | Accelerated |
|---|---|---|---|
| Start | 100% | 100% | 100% |
| Clinical advisor (0.5%) | 99.5% | 99.5% | 99.5% |
| ESOP pool (8%) | 91.5% | 91.5% | 91.5% |
| Customer-investor | 80.5% | 80.5% | 80.5% |
| Venture round (20%) | — | — | 64.4% |
| Second round (15%) | — | — | 54.7% |
| **Founder at year 7** | **~80%** | **~80%** | **~55%** |

**Read that table carefully.** The base case leaves you with four-fifths of a ₹7 crore business. The accelerated case leaves you with just over half of a ₹30 crore business. On paper the second is worth more. In practice it also comes with a board, an exit obligation, and a company you no longer fully control. **Both are legitimate. Choose on purpose.**

### 9.3 Why grants come before investors

A ₹20 lakh grant costs 0% and funds the instrument that makes the company defensible. A ₹20 lakh equity round costs 8–10% and funds whatever you spend it on. Do the free one first — but only once you have something R&D-shaped to fund, which is month 9 at the earliest [PB §7.2].

### 9.4 The financial discipline

| Rule | Why |
|---|---|
| Never spend against an unreceived grant tranche | Award letters are not cash |
| Keep 3 months of fixed costs in the bank | Service businesses die from a two-month gap |
| Customer prepayment before delivery, always | You fund the clinic's working capital otherwise |
| Revenue per hire, before the hire | If a role does not unlock ₹2× its cost in 6 months, it is a cost, not an investment |
| Separate business and personal bank accounts from day one | The most common bookkeeping disaster |

## 10. Risk, ranked by what it actually does

### 10.1 Risks that kill the company

| # | Risk | Probability | Early warning | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Completion is structurally low** — patients will not do 7 days | Medium | Week-6 completion under 40% after one redesign | Test for ₹15,000 in 6 weeks, before any real spend. Kill or redesign |
| 2 | **Founder income collapse** — you run out of money before revenue | Medium-high with ₹0 capital | Savings under 4 months and revenue under ₹50 K | Keep employment until 3 consecutive months of covering personal burn [PB §10.3] |
| 3 | **No renewals** — pilots are easy, retainers are not | Medium-high | No renewal conversation by week 5 of a cohort | Ask for the retainer when the case study lands, not later |
| 4 | **Data incident** — patient data on a personal phone | Low, catastrophic | Any patient data outside the controlled workspace | Business number, controlled sheet, DPDP-aligned consent, insurance before first patient |
| 5 | **You become the product** — delivery never transfers | Medium | You personally delivered every patient after 6 months | Coordinator at month 7, written playbook at month 9 |

### 10.2 Risks that slow the company

| # | Risk | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| 6 | A lab chain builds it in-house | They become a customer or acquirer; sell to the other chains |
| 7 | Regulatory drift — a feature becomes a device | Deliberate scope: capture, remind, format, route. Classification opinion before any new claim |
| 8 | Price pressure from a funded entrant | Never discount; move up-market to chains and licences |
| 9 | Key-person risk (illness, burnout) | Documented playbook; an advisor who can operate it for 8 weeks |
| 10 | Grant rejection burning an incubator relationship | One application per quarter; three external reviews before submitting |

### 10.3 Kill criteria, written down now

| Checkpoint | Kill if |
|---|---|
| Month 6 | Fewer than 2 paid pilots after 30+ conversations |
| Month 9 | Completion below 40% after one process redesign |
| Month 12 | Revenue below ₹75,000/month, or zero renewals |
| Month 18 | Fewer than 4 sites, or gross margin below 50% |
| Month 24 | Not profitable and not clearly on a path within 6 months |

**What "kill" means:** stop spending, return remaining capital, and move to route 1 with the assets built — 50 clinical relationships, a documented playbook, and a real understanding of a workflow. That is a funded education, and it is more than most founders have on their second attempt.

## 11. The evidence agenda and the claims ladder

### 11.1 What must be true, by when

| By | We must be able to prove | Study or source |
|---|---|---|
| Month 3 | Patients will enrol and complete a 7-day series | First paid pilot, 30 patients |
| Month 6 | Doctors act on the summary | Documented decision rate, 2 cohorts |
| Month 9 | A site will renew at a retainer price | 3 renewals |
| Month 12 | Delivery transfers to a non-founder | Coordinator-run cohort |
| Month 18 | The adequacy score agrees with clinical adjudication | 150-patient validation study [P2] |
| Month 24 | The model works in a second city | 3 sites in city two |
| Year 3 | A second condition's pathway is 70% identical | Internal analysis |
| Year 5 | Confirmation is associated with better follow-up completion | Controlled study |

**Note what is missing from that table: any claim that we lower blood pressure.** That requires a clinical trial, costs crores, and is not what we sell. We sell confirmation. If a customer wants outcome evidence, the honest answer is "that is a study we will run with a partner in year 3, and here is the design."

### 11.2 The claims ladder — what you may say at each stage

Print this. It is the single best defence against the overclaiming that ends clinical relationships.

| Stage | You MAY say | You MAY NOT say |
|---|---|---|
| **0. Today (pre-pilot)** | "We have a designed pathway, a market analysis with 44 sources, a legal scope, and a price" | Anything about outcomes, completion, sensitivity, or "clinically validated". No "AI" |
| **1. One pilot complete** | "N patients enrolled, X% completed a 7-day series, Y% led to a documented treatment decision, at [site]" | "We improve blood pressure control". Anything about other sites |
| **2. Five sites** | "Across N sites and M patients, completion is X%, and Z sites renewed at ₹Y/month" | Generalising beyond your city or your clinic type |
| **3. Instrument validated** | "The adequacy score agreed with independent clinical adjudication in N% of 150 series, with an NPV of X for the 'insufficient' call" | That it improves outcomes. That it is a medical device (unless cleared) |
| **4. Controlled study** | "Confirmation was associated with a higher rate of documented follow-up decisions" | Causation, unless the design supports it |
| **5. Regulated clearance** | Whatever the clearance permits, and not one word more | Anything outside the cleared indication |

**The rule that makes the ladder work:** when in doubt, say the lower-stage sentence. A founder who under-claims is believed about everything else.

### 11.3 What we will never claim, at any stage, without a trial

- That we lower blood pressure.
- That we prevent strokes or heart attacks.
- That we are cheaper than a doctor's visit in a way that changes outcomes.
- That our data predicts anything.
- That we are "clinically validated" before a validation study exists.

## 12. The weekly investor room

You have a standing weekly slot in front of investors. Used badly, it is the fastest way to become furniture. Used well, it is the cheapest distribution channel you will ever have.

### 12.1 The core principle

> **In a recurring room, your currency is change, not rhetoric.**

Investors see forty founders a month. The ones they remember are not the best speakers — they are the ones who came back with a number that did not exist last time. If you pitch the same story every week, by week four you are background noise, and by week eight someone will be polite to your face and ignore you after.

So the discipline is structural:

| Frequency | What you do | Duration |
|---|---|---|
| **Every week** | Attend. Ask one question. Ask for one introduction. Report nothing new | 5 minutes of their time |
| **Every 3–4 weeks** | A short update: one number moved, one thing learned, one ask | 3 minutes |
| **Every 6–8 weeks, or on a milestone** | **Pitch.** Deck, narrative, ask | 10–12 minutes |
| **Never** | Re-pitch the same deck with the same numbers | — |

### 12.2 The first nine months: the room is a distribution channel, not a funding channel

At stage 0, with no revenue and no clinical advisor, the honest probability of a cheque is close to zero — and asking for one costs you credibility you will need later.

**What this room can actually give you, in order of value:**

| Ask | Value | How to ask |
|---|---|---|
| 1. An introduction to one lab owner or clinic manager | **Highest.** This is the thing that produces revenue | "Do you know one lab owner in [city] who would take a 15-minute call? I'll do the rest" |
| 2. A critique of the pilot design | High. Prevents a wasted 6 weeks | "What would make a lab owner say no to this?" |
| 3. A name for the clinical advisor search | High. Compresses 3 months to 3 weeks | "Who is the most practical physician you know in primary care?" |
| 4. A warning about a regulatory trap | Medium | "Has anyone here sold into clinics without a device licence?" |
| 5. Money | Low, right now | Do not ask yet |

**Say this out loud in the room, once:** *"I'm not raising yet. I'm here to learn and to meet lab owners. When I have five paying sites, I'll come back and ask properly."* That sentence buys you nine months of goodwill and makes you memorable for the right reason.

### 12.3 The one-ask rule

Every session, exactly one ask. Not three.

| Week type | The one ask |
|---|---|
| Weeks 1–4 | "One introduction to a lab owner in [city]" |
| Weeks 5–8 | "Will you review my pilot proposal before I send it?" |
| Weeks 9–12 | "Do you know a physician who would review a protocol for 2 hours a month?" |
| After first pilot | "Here is the result. Who else should I show it to?" |
| After 3 sites | "I'm raising ₹25 lakh at month 12. Who should I be talking to now?" |

**Why one ask:** three asks means zero asks. The room will do the smallest thing you name, so name the one that matters.

### 12.4 The 8-week rotation (so you are never the same founder twice)

| Week | What you bring | What it proves |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The vision in five sentences and one ask | Clarity |
| 2 | One market fact they did not know (41.2% sensitivity) | You have done the reading |
| 3 | The pilot proposal, asking for critique | You listen |
| 4 | A revised proposal, naming what you changed because of their feedback | You act on advice — rare, and remembered |
| 5 | The one-pager and sample summary | You have built something |
| 6 | A conversation you had with a lab owner, verbatim | You are actually selling |
| 7 | The first paid pilot, signed | **Momentum** |
| 8 | The first completion number | **Evidence** |

**Week 4 is the most important.** Almost no founder comes back and says "you told me X, I changed it, here is the result." Doing that once makes you the founder they root for.

### 12.5 Scripts for the three conversations you will have every week

**"So what do you do?"** (the 20-second version)

> "I run the follow-up for patients whose blood pressure is high. A clinic flags them, we run a seven-day home measurement pathway, chase the ones who stop, and put a one-page summary on the doctor's desk before the follow-up. The doctor decides everything. We just make sure there's something to decide about. We're pre-revenue, running our first paid pilots now."

**"Come back when you have traction."**

> "That's fair, and it's what I expected. Can I ask you one thing before I do: which number would make this interesting to you — five paying sites, or a completion rate above 60%? I want to come back with the right one."

That question converts a rejection into a specification, and it tells you exactly what to build toward. **Write down the answer.** If five investors name the same number, that is your milestone.

**"Why won't a big lab just build this?"**

> "They might, and if they do, they'll be a customer or an acquirer — I'd rather sell to the other nine chains. What's hard to copy isn't the software, it's fifty lab owners who take my call and the completion-rate playbook. That takes two years of doing it badly first."

### 12.6 The tracker (keep this, it is worth more than the deck)

| Date | Who | What they said | What they offered | Follow-up |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| | | | | |

Four rules for the tracker:

1. **Log the exact words of the objection.** "Too early" is useless. "I don't believe clinics will pay monthly" is a testable hypothesis.
2. **Count introductions, not meetings.** Meetings are vanity. Intros are pipeline.
3. **Follow up within 48 hours**, with one line referencing what they said. Almost nobody does this.
4. **Review it monthly.** If the same objection appears five times, it is not an objection — it is a fact about your pitch.

### 12.7 The 20-no rule

You will hear "no" or "not yet" about twenty times before the first cheque. That is normal, and it is not a signal about the business — it is the base rate.

| What to count | What not to count |
|---|---|
| Named introductions to potential customers | Number of meetings |
| Objections, written verbatim | "Great conversation" |
| Weeks since the last new customer conversation | Weeks since the last pitch |
| Milestones hit | Decks made |

**The failure mode:** treating the weekly room as the scoreboard. It is not. The scoreboard is paying sites. A room full of admirers and zero customers is a hobby with better catering.

### 12.8 What never to do in that room

| Never | Why |
|---|---|
| Change the story every week | Signals you are guessing. Consistency is credibility |
| Quote a number you cannot source | One caught exaggeration ends you permanently in a recurring room |
| Say "we're the Uber of healthcare" | Marks you as unserious in a room of operators |
| Ask for money before month 9 | You will get a "no" that is hard to reverse |
| Argue with a critique in the room | Take it, thank them, test it, report back. That is how you win the room |
| Name a clinician who has not agreed to be named | Ends the relationship and the advisor |
| Bring a co-founder you met last week | Signals desperation and instability |

### 12.9 The month-12 pitch (the first one that counts)

When you have 3 sites and one renewal, the room becomes a funding channel. The document is [P4]. The differences from what you have been doing:

| Before | At month 12 |
|---|---|
| Ask: an introduction | Ask: ₹25 lakh |
| Framing: "I'm learning" | Framing: "Here is the evidence, here is the plan" |
| Deck: none, or 6 slides | Deck: 12 slides, slide 5 is traction |
| Audience: anyone in the room | Audience: 3–5 people you have spoken to ten times |
| Follow-up: within 48 hours | Follow-up: a term sheet within 2 weeks |

**You will not be pitching strangers at month 12.** You will be pitching the five people who watched you build it. That is the entire point of attending weekly for a year, and it is worth more than any amount of deck polish.

## The last page

If you read only one part of this document in a year, read this.

You are not building a smartwatch company, a health app, or an AI diagnostic. You are building **a job that nobody in Indian healthcare wants to do, done so well that clinics pay for it, and measured so honestly that it becomes evidence.**

Everything else — the grant, the pitch, the cap table, the weekly room — is machinery around that. The machinery is replaceable. The job is not.
