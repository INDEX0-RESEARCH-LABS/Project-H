# 03 — Clinical Advisor Pitch

**Reader:** one doctor you have already spoken to at least twice
**Ask:** 2 hours a month for 24 months, protocol sign-off, and the use of their name
**Give:** 0.5% equity, vesting monthly over 24 months with a 6-month cliff, plus co-authorship on the pilot report
**When:** month 2–6. **Never in a cold first message.** This is the second or third conversation, not the first.
**Format:** a one-page letter, handed over or emailed as a PDF. Not a deck.

---

## The honest starting point

0.5% of a company with no product, no revenue, and no customers is worth **₹0 today**. If the letter pretends otherwise, the doctor will see through it in four seconds and the relationship ends politely.

So the letter does not lead with equity. It leads with the three things that are genuinely valuable to a busy clinician:

1. **A dataset nobody in their department has.** How many of their patients with a high reading actually complete a home series, and what happens to the ones who don't. They cannot currently buy that number.
2. **Co-authorship.** A pilot report, and later a manuscript, with their name on it. In Indian academic medicine this is worth more than 0.5% of a pre-revenue company, and it is the honest thing to say out loud.
3. **A protocol they control, not one they inherit.** They write the clinical rules; you operate them. Most vendors hand a doctor a workflow and ask for a signature. This is the reverse.

Say that in the meeting. Then hand over the letter.

---

## The letter (print this, one page)

```
                                                        [Your name]
                                                        [Phone] · [Email]
                                                        [City] · [Date]

Dear Dr [Name],

Thank you for the two conversations about how [clinic/lab name] follows up
patients with a high blood pressure reading. You said something I have not
been able to stop thinking about: that you genuinely do not know how many of
those patients ever bring back a measurement, and that nobody has time to
find out.

I would like to build that answer with you, and I would like your help doing
it properly.

WHAT I AM DOING
I run a 7-day home BP confirmation pathway for clinics: enrolment, a
measurement protocol, reminders, follow-up calls to the patients who stop,
and a one-page summary on the treating doctor's desk before the follow-up
consultation. I have run [N] paid pilots and [M] patients through it. I do
not diagnose, advise, or prescribe. Every clinical decision belongs to the
treating doctor.

WHAT I AM ASKING
Two hours a month for 24 months, specifically:

  · Review and sign off the measurement protocol (once, then annually)
  · Take one 30-minute call a month on operational or clinical questions
  · Allow me to name you as clinical advisor when I speak to other clinics
  · Help me frame the pilot report so it is useful to clinicians, not just
    to me

That is the whole commitment. No operational work, no patient contact
responsibility, no liability for how I run the pathway.

WHAT YOU WOULD GET
  · 0.5% of the company, vesting monthly over 24 months, with a 6-month
    cliff. I want to be direct: today that is worth nothing. It is worth
    something only if this becomes a real business, and if it does not,
    you have lost two hours a month and I have lost the company.
  · Co-authorship on the pilot report, and on any manuscript that comes
    out of the data. The first report will describe what actually happens
    to high-reading patients at [clinic/lab name] — a number I do not
    believe exists anywhere in published form for a clinic like yours.
  · The protocol is yours. I operate it. If it is wrong, you change it.
  · First access to the anonymised dataset for your own teaching or
    research, subject to your institution's ethics process.

WHAT I AM NOT ASKING FOR
  · Your patients' data, beyond what your consent process permits
  · Your personal liability for my operations
  · A financial commitment of any kind
  · Your endorsement of any clinical claim. I do not make clinical claims

WHY ME
I am not a clinician, and I am not going to pretend to be one. What I have
is a working pathway, paying customers, and a record of exactly where
patients drop off. That is the half of this problem that clinicians do not
have time to solve, and it is the half I can carry.

If this is worth one more conversation, I will come to you. Fifteen minutes
is enough, and I will bring the protocol draft so you have something
concrete to react to.

With respect and thanks,

[Signature]
[Name]
```

**Why the letter is structured this way:** it opens with their own words, states the ask as a fixed time budget, names the equity's current worthlessness before they do, and gives them control of the clinical content. Every one of those four moves is doing work.

## 1. What the advisor actually does (be precise — vagueness reads as a trap)

| Activity | Hours/month | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Protocol review and sign-off | 2 (once), then 0.5/year | Without a clinician's signature the pathway has no standing in a clinic |
| Monthly 30-minute call | 0.5 | Catches operational drift before it becomes a clinical problem |
| Adjudication support on the study | 0 (year 1) / 2–4 (year 2) | Required if you later run the adequacy study in `02-GRANT.md` |
| Named reference for other clinics | 0.25 | The single most useful thing they give you; one call from them is worth twenty cold emails |
| Review of patient-facing material | 0.25 | Consent, reminder wording, escalation language |

**Total: ~2 hours/month, of which most is in the first two months.** Say this explicitly — a doctor's fear is that "advisor" means unpaid work.

### 1.1 What the advisor must never do

- Take responsibility for your operations, staff, or data.
- Act as the clinical provider for your service. The treating doctor at each clinic does that.
- Sign off on marketing claims. If you ever make a clinical claim, that needs a different, formal process.
- Become a shareholder with decision rights. 0.5% is not a board seat and must not be described as one.

## 2. The term sheet (one page, attached to the letter when they say yes)

```
CLINICAL ADVISOR — TERM SHEET (non-binding until definitive agreement)

Advisor:            Dr [Name], [qualification], [institution]
Company:            [Entity name] (or [your name], sole proprietor, until
                    incorporation — equity to be issued at incorporation)

ROLE
  Clinical advisor. Protocol review and sign-off, monthly advisory call,
  named reference, review of patient-facing material. Approximately
  2 hours per month.

EQUITY
  0.5% of fully diluted share capital, on a fully diluted basis including
  any ESOP pool.
  Vesting: monthly over 24 months.
  Cliff: 6 months. No equity vests before month 6.
  Acceleration: none.
  Exercise price: nil (issued as a grant, not options, subject to tax advice).

  If the Company has not incorporated within 12 months, this term sheet
  lapses. Equity is issued at incorporation with credit for months already
  served.

CASH
  None during the advisory term. If the Company reaches ₹50 lakh annual
  revenue, the parties will discuss a fixed annual advisory fee.

INTELLECTUAL PROPERTY
  All contributions — protocol text, adjudication criteria, study design —
  are assigned to the Company. The Advisor retains the right to use the
  protocol in their own clinical practice and teaching.

PUBLICATION
  Co-authorship on the pilot report and on any manuscript arising from
  study data, subject to journal authorship criteria and the institution's
  ethics requirements.

CONFIDENTIALITY
  Mutual. 3 years from termination. Patient data is never shared outside
  the governance described in the study protocol.

CONFLICTS
  The Advisor will disclose any competing advisory role, equity interest,
  or commercial relationship with a competing organisation, and will
  recuse from protocol decisions that affect it.

LIABILITY
  The Advisor provides advice; the Company remains responsible for its own
  operations. The Advisor is not liable for the Company's acts or omissions.
  The Company will maintain professional indemnity cover for its own
  activities.

TERMINATION
  Either party on 30 days' written notice. Vested equity is retained.
  Unvested equity is forfeited.

NOT LEGAL ADVICE
  This is a commercial summary. Both parties should have it reviewed by
  their own counsel before signing a definitive agreement.
```

**Two clauses that are not negotiable, and why:**

- **The 6-month cliff.** Without it, an advisor who takes your call twice and disappears keeps 0.5% forever. With it, they have to show up for six months before earning anything. This protects the cap table and, more importantly, tests whether they are actually engaged.
- **IP assignment.** If the protocol they write is not assigned to the company, you cannot use it with the next clinic, and you cannot put it in a grant application. This is the most commonly omitted clause and the most expensive to fix later.

## 3. Who to ask (and who not to)

### 3.1 The three profiles, ranked by usefulness to you

| Profile | Why they help | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| **A physician at a clinic that already pays you** (general medicine, internal medicine, family medicine) | They have seen the workflow fail. Their name is credible to exactly the buyers you want. Highest conversion rate | May be junior; check they can sign off protocol at their institution |
| **A community-medicine or preventive-medicine academic** | Comfortable with protocols, ethics, and data; can help design the pilot so it is publishable. Opens medical-college access | Slower; institutional sign-off needed before they can be named |
| **A cardiologist or nephrologist** | Highest status; useful for credibility with specialists and for later clinical claims | Least available, most likely to want cash, may not care about primary-care workflow. Ask only after you have revenue |

**Do not ask:** a doctor who has never referred you a patient, a doctor you have met once at a conference, or anyone who volunteers "I can introduce you to investors" in the first conversation. The last one is looking for a different deal.

### 3.2 How to ask, in the meeting

Do not send the letter cold. The sequence:

1. **They have already given you 15 minutes** and answered the five questions from the playbook.
2. **You have run at least one cohort** — even 10 patients. Ask after you have something to show, not before.
3. **You say the honest sentence:** *"I'd like to ask you to be clinical advisor. It's two hours a month. I can't pay cash, and I want to be upfront that the equity is worth nothing today. What I can offer is co-authorship on the report, and the protocol stays yours."*
4. **You hand over the letter.** Do not email it during the meeting.
5. **You do not ask for an answer in the room.** Say: "Read it, and if you want to change any of it, tell me. I'll follow up on Thursday."

**Why the follow-up date matters:** a clinician's yes in a corridor is not a yes. A dated follow-up converts.

### 3.3 The three objections, and the answers

| Objection | What it means | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| "I don't have time." | Real, and about the monthly call, not the equity | "Two hours a month, and most of it is in the first two months while we fix the protocol. After that it's a 30-minute call. If it ever exceeds that, tell me and I'll reduce it." |
| "0.5% of nothing is nothing." | They are doing the arithmetic correctly | "You're right. Today it's worth zero. The reason to do it is the dataset and the paper — and if the company works, the 0.5% is a bonus, not the point." |
| "I can't be associated with something unproven." | Reputational risk, and a fair concern | "Then let's not name you publicly until the first cohort is done. Review the protocol, and if the results are respectable, we decide about the name together." |

The third one is the important one. **Never use a clinician's name before they have seen the first results.** One premature association costs you the advisor and their network.

---

## 4. What to do if they say no

A no from a good clinician is usually about time, institutional rules, or not yet trusting you. Three responses, in order:

1. **"Would you review the protocol once, unpaid, and tell me what's wrong with it?"** This costs them 40 minutes, gives you a real clinical review, and keeps the relationship alive. About a third of these convert to advisor later.
2. **"Is there a colleague — a resident, a junior consultant — who might have more time?"** Junior clinicians are often better advisors: more available, closer to the workflow, and they become senior.
3. **"Can I come back after the first cohort with the results?"** Then actually do that, with a one-page report, in 8 weeks.

**A soft no is not a maybe.** If they have not committed after two follow-ups, move on and put them on the customer list instead. Advisors are replaceable; a wasted month is not.

## 5. Advisor red flags

Stop the conversation if any of these appear.

| Red flag | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Asks for 5–10% "because I'll open doors" | Doors are opened by results, not equity. This is a founder-tax |
| Wants to be called "co-founder" before there is a product | Co-founder is a different commitment (full-time, vesting, IP, fiduciary duty). Advisor is 2 hours/month |
| Will not sign an IP assignment | Then their protocol is not yours, and you cannot use it at the next clinic |
| Asks you to make a clinical claim they will "stand behind" | They cannot stand behind it, and you cannot evidence it |
| Wants cash now | Fine, but it means they are a consultant, not an advisor. Consultants are useful and are not equity holders |
| Wants to introduce you to investors as the main value | Wrong motivation. You need clinical judgement, not a warm intro |
| Has a competing product or advisory role and does not disclose it | Conflict you will discover at the worst time |

## 6. The 24-month advisor calendar

| Month | What happens |
|---|---|
| 1–2 | Protocol review; two 60-minute sessions; protocol v1.0 signed |
| 3 | First cohort starts; advisor is on call for escalation questions only |
| 4–5 | Advisor reviews the first summary format; one 30-minute call |
| 6 | **Cliff reached.** First 0.125% vests. Review: is this working for both of you? |
| 7–9 | Monthly 30-minute call; advisor reviews patient-facing material |
| 10–12 | Pilot report drafted; advisor reviews and is named co-author |
| 13–18 | Advisory continues; if the study goes ahead, adjudication role begins (paid separately from the grant if possible) |
| 19–24 | Final vesting; decide whether to renew at 0.25% for another 24 months, or convert to a paid advisory board seat |

**The month-6 review is the important one.** Ask directly: "Is this useful to you, and is it still two hours?" If the answer is no, end it cleanly at the cliff. That is what the cliff is for.

---

## 7. What this document deliberately does not do

- It does not promise the advisor patients, revenue, or a publication. All three depend on execution.
- It does not offer a title that implies clinical responsibility.
- It does not describe the equity as valuable. It is not, yet.
- It does not ask for money, introductions, or institutional endorsement.

The whole ask is: **two hours a month, your judgement on the protocol, and your name when the results are worth showing.** That is a small enough ask that a good clinician can say yes to it, and large enough that it changes what you can sell.

## 8. Sources and notes

- Market facts referenced in the letter's framing resolve in §16.2 of `Project-H-Market-Intelligence-Report.md`.
- Advisory equity norms (0.25–1%, 24-month vesting, 6-month cliff) are market practice, not a regulatory requirement. They are stated as convention in `Founder-Playbook-Bootstrap-India.md` §4.3.
- Equity issuance to an advisor in India requires board and shareholder approval, a valuation report in some cases, and has tax implications for the advisor (perquisite taxation on nil-price issuance). **Take CA advice before issuing.** This document is a commercial outline, not legal or tax advice.
